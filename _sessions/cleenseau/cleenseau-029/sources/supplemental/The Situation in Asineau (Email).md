# The Situation in Asineau (Email)

## Recent news

The Army of the West had been restored and reorganized. Ida Rosfeld was promoted to major and given the Enst Regiment; Colonel Leclerc became general of the Army of the West. The garrison left Asineau for Cleenseau and needed a new captain. Sabine de Brune resigned as castellan of Valit, leaving its next appointment to the future Baron of Aveil. Catherine de Brune offered Robin substantial help with Asineau in gratitude for saving Sabine, while apparently hoping he would keep Sabine's fey companion private. Eleanor, Asineau's temple steward, was seriously ill again and feared she would not survive the summer. Wymar Essford had died about two weeks earlier, after one final meeting with Rosalind.

## Land, work, and buildings

Mike described a village of roughly 300 people in a manor just under 2,000 acres: about twenty acres of village, thirty to forty acres of unusable riverbank, roughly 1,000 acres of farmland, 600 of pasture, and 200 of woodlot. The fields were marginal and under a two-year rotation, with about 500 acres sown each year and an estimated 4,000 bushels of grain in an ordinary harvest. Roughly half the able-bodied villagers worked the fields and livestock while the rest fished, though planting and harvest drew on the whole village. The palisade raised during the emergency was poorly built and needed repair or replacement. The river, about 500 feet wide here, supplied important shad and trout runs in spring.

The village had the manor house, a Wyrdling temple, and a combined mill and bakehouse. A ropemaker or netmaker worked there, along with occasional carpenters and boatbuilders; there was no blacksmith. Beer and spirits came from Beury or Cleenseau, and charcoal from Taviose. The manor house's early-1600s main block had three stories; a newer one-story extension connected it to a six-stall stable.

In the main block, the ground floor held the great hall, solar, small study, large kitchen, and attached bakehouse. The second floor held adjoining suites for the lord and partner, a guest room, a small disorganized scriptorium used by the steward, and a valuables loft. The third held two family rooms, eaves storage, and two cramped servants' dormitories. The cool cellar stored preserved food and had a thick trapdoor so it could serve as a short-term prison. The newer one-story extension had small suites for the steward and guard captain, an armory, storage rooms, and an attic servants' dormitory. Outside its six-stall stable stood a vegetable garden and chicken run. The village also had small docks and a beach on the Enst, with the Great South Road along its north side.

## Food and manorial obligations

Asineau usually had enough food but little surplus for villagers. Its economy depended on grain and fishing, with lean winters and an especially difficult period before the spring fish runs or a failed harvest. Salted or dried fish was traded locally for charcoal, beer, and other goods. Mike estimated that the manor directly held fifty acres and received a share from additional leaseholds, together bringing about 500 bushels of grain; deca on other crown-held land brought about 350 more. It was owed roughly 800 person-days of labor each year, about half used on its own land. These were contemporary estimates, not a promise of future income.

Lorin had levied charges on imported charcoal, mill or bakehouse output, fishing boats, and one fish in twenty-five from the catch. He had also sold much of the manor's food surplus, leaving ordinary villagers nearer hunger than the manorial household. The household supported about thirty people, including guards and their families, the temple staff, stablehands, Bertram and Susanna Northwoods with their child, cooks, and other servants. Robin's later tax and staffing decisions are recorded elsewhere.
