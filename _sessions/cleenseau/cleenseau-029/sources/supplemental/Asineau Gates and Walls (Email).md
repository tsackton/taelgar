## Fri, 24 Oct 2025 20:28:45 +0000 — Dan Walters <arcadinal@protonmail.com>

Subject: [Thantos] Gates and Walls - More Detail Than You Ever Wanted

A basic 'conceptual model' of the wall and a gate house, wood version before Izgil does any magicking. Note the word conceptual, this isn't full scale accurate. I'll be tweaking it as I have time to be more in line with exactly what I'm envisioning, but a private copy of AutoCAD costs close to $200/month so keep in mind this isn't any sort of engineer certified level of work. That said if Mike gives a heads up there will combat on a map like this, I can get more precise to a point.

Caveats at the moment are:

The forward ditch gradations aren't exact to what they should be, slopes would likely be more gradual realistically.
Stakes should angle forward, but that's not an option atm in this program.
Planks on the wall crisscross the wrong way and should rotate 90 degrees for more weight bearing capacity, but again that exact pattern wasn't available.
Wall should be higher and wider, I'll adjust this when I have some more time.

Front view shows the basic defensive layout. A ditch flanks the road and forces attackers either into a column along the road, or to climb the spiked slope. Bottom is close to the water table and is likely muddy, though not flooded. Heavy planks or logs buried vertically into the ground on either side of the road brace the earth to prevent a washout during heavy rains or floods.

The main fortification itself consists of heavy logs partially buried to anchor the earthen wall they're containing. The gatehouse extends slightly forward of the wall and features a set of trap doors which are bolted from the top and can be pulled open to drop things on top of troublemakers. Note that the gatehouse is designed to maximize the ability of a small number of defenders to withstand an attack from outside, but is deliberate poorly protected from the rear so as to deny an enemy a superior position against counterattack if they seize control of the wall. The nearby cart represents a typical improvised barricade that may be pushed behind the gate to further frustrate any attack.

The gatehouse is flanked by a pair of basic towers featuring an arrow slit and a top firing position that is accessed via a ladder and trap door from below. Steps from inside the town lead directly into the tower, and while an individual at the top can hold that position if the gate is breeched, it again makes it more difficult for an enemy to entrench there against a counterattack. I believe the base wall height should be twenty feet and thus should be somewhat wider as well (it's currently depicted as roughly ten feet high), so I'll expand this later when I have some time, thankfully this mapping program is scalable to a point.

Again, this is a rough conceptual layout, I'll make things more precise (as much as this particular software allows) as things go.

Daniel Walters

More stuff probably only I really care about:

Trebuchets vs. Catapults:

How siege weapons get used in Hollywood is a typical fiction is a continual source of annoyance for me, particularly ever since Peter Jackson festooned the walls of Minas Tirith with trebuchets, they've popped up in all kinds of inappropriate situations including random field battles, which is like...no...a catapult already takes some time to setup, and a trebuchet even more so as it's a much more complicated and sophisticated piece of equipment. Also armies can move, and if you see someone pointing a trebuchet in your direction than you'll just...move...they're not turrets, repositioning that kind of stuff is work.

Anyway, I've been reading up some on this stuff. There isn't a hard and fast rule on which of the two is "better" or even which really has more range for a device of equivalent size and throwing stuff of the same weight. As a general rule a similarly sized trebuchet can throw heavier stuff at about the same distance, though again requiring a lot more technical expertise. The main advantage a trebuchet gives you is a much higher arc, not necessarily a longer one. It's also had much harder to half-ass a trebuchet than a catapult and requires engineers with more skill.

A big point here is that relative height of ground matters less for trebuchets. It's much easier to hit a (stationary, again this is not a field battle weapon) target on higher ground with a trebuchet, you can throw over a defensive wall much more easily, and you get less additional range if your trebuchet is on high ground because again, it's a much higher throwing arc.

This makes a trebuchet both a solid offensive and defensive weapon in a siege type situation IF used correctly. Now, in RotK, they had trebuchets up on the walls, which actually fails to make use of their chief selling point from the defensive side, namely being that they don't need to be on top of walls. A much better strategy would have been as follows:

Place your trebuchets behind your defensive walls since they can throw over them. If the enemy has its own trebuchets they could still try to target yours, but now they can't see them which makes doing so MUCH harder. Nazgul would have had a much harder time harrying Gondorian artillery crews if they actually had to dive into the city to get at them rather than just skimming the walls. A spotter on your side in a tower or on a wall will be able to see enemy siege weapons just fine, however, and runners can relay the position of enemy equipment back to their artillery crews and can inform them how to adjust based on where their shots are going.

Also, a brief note on incendiaries. Hollywood of course loves having medieval artillery lobbing stuff that explodes because it looks cool (see the napalm using Romans in Gladiator for details). In real life of course setting stuff on fire at range, much less making it explode, is a lot of work and extremely expensive. It's not that you couldn't launch incendiaries from a catapult or trebuchet, but why would you? The cost is high (oil and its derivatives have a lot of uses and isn't cheap) and the return on investments is probably low.

Some extra oily rags does nothing against a stone structure that the rock they were wrapped around already didn't, and I suppose you could set some thatched roofs on fire assuming everything is nice and dry...but again, why? That's a very expensive use of a limited resource that's not easily replenished, especially if you somehow manage to actually burn the place you're attacking, you can't loot it for supplies. Hollywood has a habit of vastly downplaying the importance of logistics and supplies in warfare, most likely because it's boring and most people don't care.

Daniel Walters
