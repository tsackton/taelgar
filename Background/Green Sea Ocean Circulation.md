# Green Sea Ocean Circulation

This is a working ocean model designed to accompany [[Taelgar Climatic Model]] and [[Trading in the Green Sea]]. It covers the [[Green Sea]], the [[Western Gulf]], and their exchange with the [[Outer Ocean]]. It establishes broad patterns of circulation, temperature, and marine productivity, but leaves detailed channels and local navigation open for future adventure design and worldbuilding. This is intended as guide to organize invention. 

## Overview

**Cold water enters in the northeast, warm water enters in the southeast, and their branches feed a shared eastward outflow offshore between Ursk and Irrla.**

The northern coastal current is relatively narrow and fast, especially under the summer easterlies, when the winds reinforce the current. The offshore outflow is broader, and its surface flow slows substantially when winds oppose it, though water continues returning east through a combination of surface and deeper routes. The southern inflow is broad and meandering around coastal headlands and likely branches.

## Major Currents

Three major currents define the Green Sea circulation: two inflows, one cold in the north, and one warm in the south, plus a shared mixed outflow. The entrance and exit arrows on the map above identify regional exchange sectors, but aren't intended to indicate specific navigable gaps or precise water movements. This model requires adequate eastern outlet/exchange capacity and a suitable pressure pattern, which is assumed to exist but not developed in detail. For now, currents are described phenomenologically, but eventually could have in-world names. 

### Cold northern inflow

A branch of cold Outer Ocean water enters through the northern Eastern Isles and moves west along the northern margin of the Green Sea, past [[Ursk]] toward the northwestern basin. It occupies the upper ocean, with cooler water also extending beneath the seasonally warmed surface. Its influence changes as it mixes and exchanges heat along the route.

The main westward flow stays fairly close to shore. Summer easterlies reinforce it, increasing its speed. Near Skaerhem, islands, banks, and headlands divide and redirect the current, making navigation difficult in places; the exact locations remain for local worldbuilding. A portion of the northern water turns offshore to feed the confluence rather than continuing into the Western Gulf.

### Warm southern inflow

Warm upper-ocean water enters through gaps in the southern island chain and spreads west through the southern Green Sea. A branch turns north through the western basin, west of [[Irrla]], carrying warmth toward Cymea and the confluence. 

Southern spring and summer easterlies reinforce the westward surface flow. The warm inflow maintains a southern and western heat reservoir, while its temperature is progressively modified by local weather and mixing as it turns north on the western edge of [[Irrla]]. 

### Shared offshore outflow

The branches approach one another off northern Cymea, toward the western end of the corridor between Ursk and Irrla. Their water moves east along a broad **ocean front**, a boundary between water masses with different temperature and salinity. Mixing continues downstream: the outflow contains warmer and cooler bands and eddies, rather than becoming uniformly lukewarm at one junction.

The main return crosses the open water north of Irrla and leaves through several eastern passages. The division of this outflow between surface passages and channels beneath the moving islands remains undecided.

> [!tip] Why the outflow can continue when surface winds oppose it
> Wind acts most directly on the upper water. The broader circulation also responds to differences in sea level and density, shaped by the two inflows, heating, cooling, and basin geometry. Summer easterlies can slow the offshore surface current considerably while deeper or more widely distributed eastward flow continues. Over time, total inflow and outflow must balance, allowing for rivers, evaporation, and precipitation; the basin cannot simply accumulate water throughout the summer.

## Temperatures and seasonal changes

Water temperature reflects imported heat, sunlight, heat lost to the atmosphere, and mixing between depths. Winter cooling supplies additional cold northern water; southern inflow and summer sunshine add heat. 

The northern coastal current can stay cool while sheltered bays warm considerably in summer. Well-mixed Skaer banks warm less rapidly than a thin, sheltered surface layer. The shallow Western Gulf can have a substantial seasonal temperature range, interrupted by cooler patches along its upwelling coasts. The outflow's temperature depends on the proportions and histories of both inflows.

> [!tip] Why summer winds strengthen the northern coastal current
> Along a south-facing Northern Hemisphere coast, winds blowing toward the west favor net surface-layer transport toward shore. Coastal accumulation and the resulting pressure slope can reinforce a westward current. This **downwelling-favorable** tendency is compatible with a cold current arriving from elsewhere; it does not imply that the water must be warm or that this coast is undergoing summer upwelling.

## The Western Gulf

The Western Gulf exchanges water with the larger sea through the [[Straits of Cymea]]. 

The rivers of [[Sembara]] and [[Tyrwingha]], together with the [[Volta]] flowing through [[Tollen]], supply a substantial freshwater surplus: river inflow and rainfall exceed evaporation. Fresher, less dense water spreads across the surface and flows outward through the straits, while cold, saltier water enters beneath it. The straits are very deep, accommodating both layers of exchange. The outward flow carries both the returning marine water and the gulf's freshwater surplus.

The narrowness of the straits concentrates the surface outflow into a strong current, with eddies along its margins. Sailing ships leaving the gulf gain current assistance, while entering ships must make headway against it. Limited room to tack makes a favorable wind especially important for inward passage; in calms or contrary winds, sailing vessels wait for better conditions. Winds opposing the outflow steepen the waves, and winds and tides can alter the current's strength and sometimes reverse local flow. Local pilotage and careful handling are important in both directions, making the straits a more demanding passage than the sheltered waters within the gulf. 

Along western Cymea, the southward component of the summer winds drives surface water away from west-facing shores. Cooler, nutrient-rich water rises to replace it, creating productive coastal waters along the eastern side of the gulf. The strength and location of this upwelling vary with the seabed and coastline. Along the opposing east-facing shores, the same wind regime pushes surface water toward land and drives it downward. Summer upwelling is therefore concentrated on the Cymean side of the gulf.

The gulf's rare deep canyons are possible sites for connections to the [[Elemental Plane of Water]], as discussed under [[#Optional magical contribution|below]]. 

## Marine productivity

The name of the Green Sea reflects rich phytoplankton in its western waters, which arises through several mechanisms: river nutrients, winter mixing, nutrient recycling in shallow water, mixing around banks and passages, and localized seasonal upwelling.

### Magical contributions to productivity

There is some brainstorming and tentative hints that there are occasional deep canyon connections to the [[Elemental Plane of Water]], particularly in the Western Gulf. One potential ecological role for these connections is via persistent upward mixing of nutrient-bearing seawater, sustaining an unusually reliable feeding ground when ordinary seasonal upwelling weakens. Its depth, direction, and surrounding terrain must allow that influence to reach sunlit water; simply adding water far below the surface would not suffice. However, this could produce a situation where, at least in some places, unusually productive fishing grounds, for example, could be the result of elemental connections -- and potentially a place where elemental monsters transit to the material plane for feeding opportunities. 

## Local navigation

The fast coastal branch makes northwestern navigation difficult and local knowledge valuable. The same current that helps a ship travel west can carry it rapidly toward a shoal, divide at an island, or leave a vessel in an unexpected eddy. Local knowledge includes when to enter the current, which passages to avoid, and where to stand offshore. These conditions support exceptional Skaer seamanship.

[[Trading in the Green Sea]] develops route choices, sailing calendars, and commercial consequences.

## Parameters left open

- Current speeds, widths, transport volumes, salinities, temperatures, and the vertical division of eastern exchange.
- The exact confluence position, seasonal movement, and degree of summer weakening of the offshore surface flow.
- Shelf edges, Skaer banks, navigable channels, tidal strength, and the local divisions of the coastal current. Map arrows cannot establish safe passages.
- The detailed strength and onward route of the Outer Ocean's warm and cold currents.
- Local sea ice, harbor opening dates, and the reach of any port magic. See [[Sea Ice in the Green Sea]] for brainstorming. 
- Whether to adopt extraplanar inflows, and what exceptional local feature it would explain.

## Physical comparisons and references

These real-world examples explain the physical mechanisms used in the model and provide comparisons for further worldbuilding.

- [JMA: Oyashio](https://www.data.jma.go.jp/kaiyou/data/db/hakodate/knowledge/oyashio.html): a cold northern water source, fronts, seasonal reach, and substantial transport without exceptionally fast surface flow.
- [Yasuda et al., 1996: North Pacific Intermediate Water](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/phoc/26/4/1520-0485_1996_026_0448_damonp_2_0_co_2.xml): northern and southern water masses mixing while moving east through a frontal region.
- [NOAA: what drives currents](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/current.html) and [NASA: ocean topography](https://sealevel.jpl.nasa.gov/news/1126/dynamic-ocean-topography-with-current-arrows/): winds, density, sea-level differences, and rotation.
- [Keafer et al., 2005: coastal transport in the Gulf of Maine](https://www.whoi.edu/fileserver.do?id=35329&p=28251&pt=2): downwelling-favorable winds can strengthen and confine coastal transport.
- [NOAA: winter mixing](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/science-blog/winter-sets-oceans-clocks) and [coastal upwelling](https://www.psl.noaa.gov/news/2021/060821.html): seasonal nutrient replenishment and the importance of coastal orientation.
- [NOAA: Georges Bank](https://www.integratedecosystemassessment.noaa.gov/regions/northeast/georges-bank): productive shallow banks supporting cod, haddock, and whale feeding grounds.
- [NOAA: Northeast currents](https://www.integratedecosystemassessment.noaa.gov/regions/northeast/northeast-currents): shallow and deeper northern water sources; cold water is not necessarily richer in nutrients than warmer water.
- [WHOI: Where the whales are](https://www.whoi.edu/ocean-learning-hub/multimedia/where-the-whales-are/): fronts can concentrate prey into feeding patches.
- [NOAA: sea-state forecasting](https://www.ndbc.noaa.gov/faq/sea_state_forecasting.shtml): waves opposing currents become steeper.
