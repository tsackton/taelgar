# Climate of the Sentinels and Northern Highlands

The highlands separate milder western forests and eastern lowlands with a broad belt of cold peaks, snowy slopes, sheltered valleys, and dry interior plateaus. Latitude lengthens winter toward the north; elevation makes high terrain cold even in the southern range. Ocean exposure and mountain shelter govern moisture separately from those temperature differences. A dry basin can retain winter snow, while a wet lower western slope receives rain and repeated thaws.

## Regional coverage

- **Sentinel high country:** the northern, central, and southern [[Sentinel Range]], including high slopes, valleys, passes, snowfields, and glaciers.
- **Northern highlands:** [[Zimkova]], including [[Ardlas]] and [[Breva]], the [[Pekul Mountains]], and the [[Vostok Plateau]] and adjoining interior Vostok uplands.

This volume follows mountain elevation, exposure, and the transition into interior plateaus. Western lowland forests and forested foothills belong in [[Climate of the Western Coast and Interior]]; the [[Aurbez Plateau]] belongs in [[Climate of Greater Sembara and Aurbez]]. Coastal Vostok and the Svinjo–Yengorza country belong in [[Climate of the Green Sea and Its Shores]].

## The main climatic contrasts

Across much of the [[Sentinel Range]], western slopes receive more precipitation, especially in winter. This contrast has an important summer exception: maritime air from the Green Sea supplies rain to the Sembaran eastern foothills and districts such as [[Ardlas]]. The reach of that moisture varies along the range. It does not make every eastern basin rainy or carry a uniform wet summer through to interior Vostok and the southern high country.

For a particular location, distinguish its **height, exposure, and position within a valley**. An exposed crest can lose snow to wind while a hollow below accumulates deep drifts. A low sheltered valley can have a colder clear night than a nearby slope, yet still have a longer growing season than the high peaks. Snow falling in a storm, snow remaining after a thaw, and permanent ice are different boundaries; none has an assigned elevation in this atlas.

The mechanisms and seasonal maps are developed in [[Sentinels and Continental Weather Connections]]. Local weather sequences and practical implications remain model guidance; exact temperature normals, snowlines, and pass-opening dates remain unassigned.

## Sentinel high country

### Northern range and lower intervening country

The [[Northern Sentinels|northern range]] has long, severe winters and short summers. Exposed high terrain gathers substantial snow, while glaciers and persistent snowfields remain in high valleys and sheltered upper slopes. The peaks need not be as tall as the central range to retain ice: the colder northern setting and shorter melt season also matter. Lower wooded slopes can thaw and support summer growth beneath still-snowy heights.

Around the transition southward toward the central range, lower mountains and valleys offer a less severe setting than the highest northern peaks. Their seasonal conditions depend on shelter, snow storage, and local access to maritime air. Some lower routes may be usable when high routes are blocked, but this is not an established year-round crossing network. A climatic description alone cannot establish the availability or safety of a particular route.

The western forested foothills and [[Blackwater Fens]] belong in [[Climate of the Western Coast and Interior]]. Blackwater's long, cold, wet winter can include local open waterways and thaws without setting the conditions in the snowy mountain valleys above it. The ordinary mountain baseline also leaves the exceptional conditions around Pandar and the Forest of Nightmares separate.

### Central range

The central Sentinels rise above comparatively milder forested foothills into steep rocky country, glaciers, and high icefields. Winter storms deliver substantial snow on exposed western slopes; sheltered eastern basins receive less, with Green Sea summer moisture reaching suitable eastern approaches. The central high country retains snow and ice through summer even when adjacent lower valleys are warm.

This is a strong vertical change over a journey rather than one temperature for the whole range. A traveler can leave rain in a forest valley, climb into wet snow, and reach cold wind and persistent ice higher up. Wind, cloud, and snow conditions can alter quickly as the route turns from a sheltered slope to a crest. The Gazetteer's difficult and poorly known high terrain remains difficult even during a settled summer interval; summer weather does not create a general open crossing.

The desolation around [[Urlich Pass]] is a separate geographical and magical limit. This atlas supplies no ordinary weather explanation for its devastation and does not infer that a dry or snow-free period restores the former route.

### Southern range

The southern Sentinels have warmer lower valleys and a longer warm season than the north, but their high passes and summits remain cold. Much of the upper country is dry and barren, with more sheltered woodland below. Winter snow closes higher passes; most seasonal snow melts during the warmer months, while some glaciers and permanent snowfields persist around the highest peaks.

The contrast is between **reduced moisture** and **complete absence of snow**. Passing winter weather can deposit enough snow to block a pass, and a spring or autumn snowfall can interrupt a journey outside the core winter season. Occasional summer storms reach the foothills or higher ground without turning the southern range into an extension of Dunmar's wet monsoon districts.

On the eastern side, the Aurbez connection leads toward elevated, dry country with important spring rain and episodic summer storms. Farther south, routes descend toward much warmer Dunmari lowlands. The adjacent climates are in [[Climate of Greater Sembara and Aurbez]] and [[Climate of Dunmar and the Nevos]]; their warmer days do not determine conditions on the intervening crest.

## Zimkova, Pekul, and interior Vostok

[[Zimkova]] includes river depressions, wooded hills, higher plateaus, and mountain spurs. It should not be treated as one high, level surface. Lower valleys have a warmer growing season than the ridges above them, while still being subject to cold nights and long winter intervals. Bogs, lakes, and wet woods can reflect drainage, stored water, and slow drying as well as rainfall; their presence does not require uniformly heavy precipitation throughout the year.

Ardlas's established barley, oats, rye, root crops, and livestock fit its short, cool growing season and summer moisture. This supports a useful agricultural valley climate, not a crop prescription for all Zimkova or the high passes. Cultivated clearings, forest pasture, and exposed uplands have different limits even within Ardlas. The dry plateau and adjoining northern grasslands likewise need not share the rainfall of the more maritime eastern foothills.

The named geographic notes for Pekul and the Vostok Plateau remain sparse. Their treatment here follows the fixed overview's cold interior pattern and the broader geography in [[Vostok]]. Exact vegetation limits, arable extent, winter precipitation amounts, and individual valley conditions await local development. The adjoining cold, dry northern grassland character is a revisable target under K10, not an independently established regional rain calendar.

## Four seasons across the highlands

### Winter

On the western Sentinel slopes, repeated disturbances bring snow to high terrain and rain or wet snow farther down. Crimson and Elderwood's broad low foothills have naturally mild, predominantly rainy winters; their adopted summer magic supplies no winter warming. Northern and central snow cover persists between passages, though the wind redistributes it. A sheltered hollow can have deep snow while a neighboring ridge is scoured. Milder western incursions can produce a lower-slope thaw without removing the established icefields above.

East of the crest, cold continental air and reduced moisture favor clearer, drier intervals. Ardlas valleys can hold frost and fog; Breva retains bitter cold and lasting snow. Exposed interior plateaus can be windy while a nearby basin is still and intensely cold. Moving disturbances interrupt these spells with snow, and an occasional downslope warming can change conditions in selected valleys without ending winter across the region.

The higher southern passes close under snow even while lower Aurbez or Dunmari districts remain more accessible. Their snow distribution depends on recent storms and terrain. No amount of dry weather elsewhere establishes that a high pass is clear.

### Spring

Thaw begins unevenly. Sunny lower slopes lose seasonal snow while shaded gullies and high basins retain it; higher storms can continue to add snow as meltwater swells streams below. Wet ground and rising water can complicate a valley journey even during fine weather. A warm interval followed by renewed snow can reverse recent improvements on higher routes.

Ardlas and lower Zimkovan valleys gain usable warmth before exposed ridges and the colder northern plateaus. Maritime incursions and passing fronts bring unsettled weather as the eastern summer moisture pattern develops. Farther south, spring wet spells and mountain meltwater feed adjoining Aurbez streams despite the drier summer ahead. There is no single thaw date for the highlands or uniform order of opening among named passes.

### Summer

Low valleys can be mild or warm while crests remain cold, windy, and partly snow-covered. Northern and central glaciers persist through periods of melting, and sufficiently cold high terrain can receive fresh snow during a disturbance. Clear weather can improve visibility and conditions on some approaches without removing the underlying obstacles of ice, steep relief, and poorly known routes.

Ardlas and exposed Sembaran approaches receive summer rain, ranging from showers to longer cloudy wet spells. Sheltered interior basins and the Vostok Plateau are less reliably watered. On the western side, Crimson and Elderwood share Chasa and Chardon's predominantly dry Mediterranean summer atmosphere, while adopted elven magic supplies or enhances moisture within the forests through recurring mist, canopy drip and damp soil; its exact operation and bounds remain unresolved. Weaker magical influence and Fiatara shelter toward Highveil produce more open, drier woodland before the continental northern forests. A storm on one slope need not imply the same conditions across the crest.

Southern high country has longer dry intervals. Lower valleys enjoy a warmer season, but cold nights and exposed high terrain remain important. Occasional thunderstorms or other moist incursions can interrupt a journey; they do not supply a dependable wet season across the range.

### Autumn

Cooling rebuilds seasonal snow first where height, shade, and northern latitude favor persistence. A first snowfall may disappear from a sunny slope but remain on a shaded crossing. Lower woods can still have autumn color while the peaks are white, matching the retained northern mountain travel observations from late September and early October without fixing an annual onset.

Western rain-bearing weather becomes more active. In the east, summer moisture gives way to more frequent cold outbreaks and passing fronts. Frost returns to interior valleys, and snow lasts progressively longer in Breva and the northern uplands. Southern valleys remain relatively warm longer, yet high routes can deteriorate well before winter becomes persistent below. Thawing rain on old snow, renewed freezing, and a subsequent clear spell are all compatible with this transition.

## Recurring weather and practical use

The following are model-based sequences for local scenes and journeys. Their physical explanations and seasonal maps are maintained in [[Sentinels and Continental Weather Connections#Recurring mountain weather episodes]].

| Situation | Conditions along a journey | Practical consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Western storm, eastern shelter | Wet low western slopes give way to high snow, with clearer or drier conditions descending into selected eastern valleys. | A dry destination does not imply a dry crossing; snow and cloud can remain concentrated along the crest. |
| Winter valley cold pool | Clear calm weather leaves frost or fog in a low basin while nearby slopes are brighter or temporarily warmer. | Valley weather should not be copied directly to a ridge, or ridge sunshine used to remove valley frost. |
| Eastern summer wet spell | Moist air reaches Ardlas and exposed foothills, producing showers or prolonged rain. | Field growth and river supply benefit, while wet tracks and cloud can impede movement; sheltered interior districts may receive less. |
| Spring thaw followed by snow | Mild days raise streams and reduce lower snow, then colder weather rebuilds snow higher up. | A recently passable stretch can become difficult again; melt below does not establish an opening date above. |
| High-country summer interruption | Fine valley weather gives way to cold cloud, strong wind, rain, hail, or high snow during a disturbance. | A summer journey can still encounter wintry conditions without implying an unusually cold whole season. |
| Autumn elevation contrast | A mild lower-valley day follows a cold night; old snow remains in shaded high country and a later passage adds more. | Conditions can worsen upslope before lowland vegetation or weather suggests winter. |

For fields and grazing, the atlas distinguishes a usable valley growing season from high terrain where snow persists. It does not assign new agricultural settlements, crops, or seasonal grazing routes outside the established evidence. Ardlas provides the clearest developed example; sparse northern plateaus and high Sentinel country require their own local checks. Likewise, mountain snowmelt can support rivers downstream through a dry interval without making every intervening district rainy.

For travel, height and exposure are as important as the season's name. Higher southern pass closures are required. Possible lower winter routes between northern and central sectors remain a separate, revisable worldbuilding preference. Wellby's Aurbez–Dunmar backstory concerns the southern range and supplies no early opening date or evidence for northern winter access. Neither ordinary surface weather nor this atlas defines conditions on underground Dwarven roads.

## Evidence limits and adjoining regions

K08–K10 and K12–K14 in [[Climate and Weather Constraints]] retain Ardlas's short mild summer and summer rainfall, Breva's bitter snowy winter, Vostok's cold dry interior, the Sentinel slope contrast, high glaciers, and southern pass closures. E08 and E10 preserve ordinary winter and autumn examples; they do not define a full seasonal calendar. Glacier extent, snow duration, mountain heights used in climate calculations, favored storm faces, and precise rainfall boundaries remain open.

The [[Svinjo Mountains]], [[Isenborg]], and [[Yengorza Mountains]] are separate northern Green Sea highlands. Their ordinary glaciers and observed natural whiteout belong in [[Climate of the Green Sea and Its Shores]], with shared snow and exposure mechanisms explained in [[Sentinels and Continental Weather Connections#Northern Green Sea highlands]]. They must not inherit the Sentinel western/eastern slope contrast automatically.

Magical Great Chasm weather, supernatural cold, deep ice habitats, and the desolation at Urlich supply no ordinary temperature or precipitation norm. Lavnoch's terrain can clarify a connection but adds no separate climate requirement. The proposed [[Sentinels and Continental Weather Connections#Winter storms from Chardon to the Green Sea|winter storm redevelopment]] explains a possible connection to eastern lowland rain; it is not a fixed mountain storm track or another regional season.

Western forest approaches are covered in [[Climate of the Western Coast and Interior]], the southern plateau in [[Climate of Greater Sembara and Aurbez]], coastal Vostok in [[Climate of the Green Sea and Its Shores]], and the Dunmari descent in [[Climate of Dunmar and the Nevos]].
