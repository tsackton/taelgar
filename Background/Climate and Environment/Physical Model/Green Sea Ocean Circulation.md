# Green Sea Ocean Circulation

This working ocean model accompanies [[Taelgar Climate Model]], [[Green Sea Atmospheric Circulation]], and [[Trading in the Green Sea]]. It covers the [[Green Sea]], the [[Western Gulf]], and their exchange with the [[Outer Ocean]]. The broad current pattern and Western Gulf exchange are adopted model choices. Seasonal expressions and physical explanations organize further invention; they do not become independent climate constraints. Detailed channels, current strengths, and local navigation remain open.

## Overview

**Cold water enters in the northeast, warm water enters in the southeast, and their branches feed a shared eastward outflow offshore between Ursk and Irrla.** The eastern exchanges can operate at several depths through and beneath the moving [[Eastern Isles]]. A surface obstruction to ships need not stop the sea's water exchange.

The circulation map describes broad pathways. The seasonal exchange view distinguishes changes in upper-water flow from the persistent basin-scale return and shows the Western Gulf's two layers. Neither establishes a navigable channel, an ice boundary, or a measured transport volume.

The northern coastal current is relatively narrow and fast, especially under the summer easterlies, when the winds reinforce the current. The offshore outflow is broader, and its surface flow slows substantially when winds oppose it, though water continues returning east through a combination of surface and deeper routes. The southern inflow is broad and meandering around coastal headlands and likely branches.

## Major Currents

Three major currents define the Green Sea circulation: two inflows, one cold in the north, and one warm in the south, plus a shared mixed outflow. Entrance and exit arrows identify regional exchange sectors, rather than specific navigable gaps or precise water movements. This model requires adequate eastern outlet/exchange capacity and a suitable pressure pattern, which is assumed to exist but not developed in detail. For now, currents are described phenomenologically, but eventually could have in-world names.

### Cold northern inflow

A branch of cold Outer Ocean water enters through the northern Eastern Isles and moves west along the northern margin of the Green Sea, past [[Ursk]] toward the northwestern basin. It occupies the upper ocean, with cooler water also extending beneath the seasonally warmed surface. Its influence changes as it mixes and exchanges heat along the route.

The main westward flow stays fairly close to shore. Summer easterlies reinforce it, increasing its speed. Near Skaerhem, islands, banks, and headlands divide and redirect the current, making navigation difficult in places; the exact locations remain for local worldbuilding. A portion of the northern water turns offshore to feed the confluence rather than continuing into the Western Gulf.

### Warm southern inflow

Warm upper-ocean water enters through gaps in the southern island chain and spreads west through the southern Green Sea. A branch turns north through the western basin, west of [[Irrla]], carrying warmth toward Cymea and the confluence. 

Southern spring and summer easterlies reinforce the westward surface flow. The warm inflow maintains a southern and western heat reservoir, while its temperature is progressively modified by local weather and mixing as it turns north on the western edge of [[Irrla]]. 

### Shared offshore outflow

The branches approach one another off northern Cymea, toward the western end of the corridor between Ursk and Irrla. Their water moves east along a broad **ocean front**, a boundary between water masses with different temperature and salinity. Mixing continues downstream: the outflow contains warmer and cooler bands and eddies, rather than becoming uniformly lukewarm at one junction.

The main return crosses the open water north of Irrla and leaves through several eastern passages. The northbound feeder west of Irrla and its junction with the northern branch remain broad corridors, with their exact paths unassigned. The division of this outflow between surface passages and channels beneath the moving islands remains undecided.

> [!tip] Why the outflow can continue when surface winds oppose it
> Wind acts most directly on the upper water. The broader circulation also responds to differences in sea level and density, shaped by the two inflows, heating, cooling, and basin geometry. Summer easterlies can slow the offshore surface current considerably while deeper or more widely distributed eastward flow continues. Over time, total inflow and outflow must balance, allowing for rivers, evaporation, and precipitation; the basin cannot simply accumulate water throughout the summer. This balance requires a return, but does not by itself prove a particular deep current or channel. See [NOAA on current drivers](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/current.html).

## Temperatures and seasonal changes

Water temperature reflects imported heat, sunlight, heat lost to the atmosphere, and mixing between depths. Winter cooling supplies additional cold northern water; southern inflow and summer sunshine add heat. 

The northern coastal current can stay cool while sheltered bays warm considerably in summer. Well-mixed Skaer banks warm less rapidly than a thin, sheltered surface layer. The shallow Western Gulf can have a substantial seasonal temperature range, interrupted by cooler patches along its upwelling coasts. The outflow's temperature depends on the proportions and histories of both inflows.

### Winter

Northern westerly intervals generally assist offshore eastward surface flow and oppose the westward coastal branch. The imported cold-water supply persists, although a storm can temporarily alter, divert, or reverse local surface flow. Cooling and wind mix the northern upper ocean, bringing deeper nutrients upward where the density structure allows it. The southern inflow and eastern exchange continue during atmospheric calms; weak wind is not a stoppage of the ocean circulation.

Exposed northern coasts remain cool, but open water releases heat and moisture into cold air. Maritime thaws and snowfall downwind can therefore coexist with a cold current and much colder inland districts. Along a persistent westward current, an eastward-moving wave field driven by westerlies can become steeper; a wind that helps an eastbound ship need not give it a smooth sea.

The Western Gulf cools and loses some summer thermal layering. River freshwater can still maintain a surface-to-depth density contrast, so winter mixing does not necessarily homogenize the entire gulf or remove its net two-layer exchange. Its relative shelter remains: normal winter cooling and occasional windy passages need not mean frequent severe storms.

### Spring

Increasing light and replenished nutrients support spring growth, although deep or vigorous mixing can delay a bloom by carrying plankton away from sunlight. Sheltered shallow water warms ahead of exposed banks and the northern coastal branch. River runoff can freshen and stratify coastal water; the strength and timing depend on each catchment's rainfall and thaw rather than one synchronous basin-wide melt.

Northern westerly opportunities continue to assist offshore eastbound surface flow. Southern easterlies begin reinforcing westward upper-water movement, and the warm branch carries water north through the western basin. These currents connect different water masses rather than establishing one sea temperature. Warm moist air crossing cold northern water or cool banks can produce fog even during otherwise useful sailing weather.

In the Western Gulf, increasing sunlight, river nutrients, and sheltered coastal water favor renewed growth. A larger freshwater pulse can strengthen near-surface stratification and alter the outflow, but no single peak-current month is assigned. Local tides, wind, and the stage of each river continue to matter at the straits.

### Summer

The northeastern atmospheric ridge and Cymean offshore trough favor E–NE winds along the northern sea. These strengthen and confine the westward coastal branch. Where the same winds oppose the offshore eastward return, its surface expression weakens markedly. Broader or deeper return continues; the division is unassigned. The central and southern sea can have different local winds around the eastern ocean high, so the degree of opposition varies across the outflow.

Warm surface layers develop in sheltered bays and quieter offshore water while mixed banks and the imported coastal branch remain cooler. The distinction helps preserve cool northern coasts alongside much warmer inland valleys and locally warm shallows. Where a warm humid air mass crosses cold water, fog or low cloud is possible. Passing disturbances stir the surface and displace fronts; persistent fine weather restores layering where turbulence is weak.

Western Cymea has adopted summer upwelling, supplying cool, nutrient-bearing water to particular coastal sectors within the warming gulf. The same broad wind regime favors downwelling along opposing shores. The detailed plume from the straits can meet the main-sea currents and form shifting fronts and eddies, without requiring one fixed convergence line.

### Autumn

Returning northern westerlies assist offshore eastward surface flow and remove the summer wind assistance to the coastal branch. The westward current can persist through that change, producing rough water where waves oppose it. Stronger disturbances and surface cooling erode summer layering, returning nutrients toward the sunlit surface; shorter days and excessive mixing eventually limit growth despite abundant nutrients.

Stored heat delays autumn cooling near deeper and warmer water. Inland frost and upland snow can arrive while adjacent open water supports mild coastal rain. Shallow sheltered bays can cool quickly in a cold spell, so the general maritime delay does not make every harbor warm. The relative contributions of imported heat, summer storage, and local mixing vary around the sea.

The Western Gulf cools, its thermal layers weaken, and rainy episodes can renew freshwater input. Surface export and deeper inflow retain their adopted net directions, with changing wind and tide producing local departures. Ordinary autumn circulation within the gulf is less constrained than the strait exchange; no clockwise or counterclockwise gulf-wide gyre is assigned.

> [!tip] Why summer winds strengthen the northern coastal current
> In the Northern Hemisphere, wind-driven transport integrated through the surface layer is directed to the right of the wind. Along a south-facing coast, winds blowing toward the west therefore favor net transport toward shore. Coastal accumulation and the resulting pressure slope can reinforce a westward current. This **downwelling-favorable** tendency is compatible with a cold current arriving from elsewhere; it does not imply that the water must be warm or that this coast is undergoing summer upwelling.

## The Western Gulf

> [!image|hero]
> ![[assets/worldbuilding/taelgar-western-gulf-exchange-v1.png]]
> *The geographic view shows the shared strait corridor and western Cymean summer upwelling. The unscaled section separates fresher surface export from deeper cold, saltier inflow; it assigns no depth, layer thickness, or current speed.*

The Western Gulf exchanges water with the larger sea through the [[Straits of Cymea]]. 

The rivers of [[Sembara]] and [[Tyrwingha]], together with the [[Volta]] flowing through [[Tollen]], supply a substantial freshwater surplus: river inflow and rainfall exceed evaporation. Fresher, less dense water spreads across the surface and flows outward through the straits, while cold, saltier water enters beneath it. The straits are very deep, accommodating both layers of exchange. The outward flow carries both the returning marine water and the gulf's freshwater surplus. Exchange and mixing transfer salt and heat between layers; the model does not make the whole surface fresh or the entire gulf equally cold.

The narrowness of the straits concentrates the surface outflow into a strong current, with eddies along its margins. Sailing ships leaving the gulf gain current assistance, while entering ships must make headway against it. Limited room to tack makes a favorable wind especially important for inward passage; in calms or contrary winds, sailing vessels wait for better conditions. Winds opposing the outflow steepen the waves, and winds and tides can alter the current's strength and sometimes reverse local flow. Local pilotage and careful handling are important in both directions, making the straits a more demanding passage than the sheltered waters within the gulf. 

Along western Cymea, the southward component of the summer winds drives surface water away from west-facing shores. Cooler, nutrient-rich water rises to replace it, creating productive coastal waters along the eastern side of the gulf. The strength and location of this upwelling vary with the seabed and coastline. Along the opposing east-facing shores, the same wind regime pushes surface water toward land and drives it downward. Summer upwelling is therefore concentrated on the Cymean side of the gulf. This is the coastline-orientation dependence described in [NOAA’s coastal upwelling explanation](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_currents/03coastal4.html); it does not make every monsoon coast an upwelling coast.

The gulf's rare deep canyons are possible sites for connections to the [[Elemental Plane of Water]], as discussed [[#Magical contributions to productivity|below]].

## Sea ice and freshwater freezing

The adopted bounds are narrower than an annual sea-ice map: the Western Gulf does not freeze as a whole; exceptional winters can freeze protected harbors; the December Skaer journey encountered no substantial sea ice. Pyhlla's regular river freezing and Ursk's cold rivers do not require comparable freezing of exposed salt water.

Cold water is not necessarily at its freezing point, and continued exchange can replace heat lost at the surface. Exposed currents and mixing help only while a source of warmer water remains available; motion alone does not prevent freezing. Shallow, sheltered, relatively fresh water can freeze sooner than deeper, exposed marine water. Once formed, drift ice follows both winds and currents. These principles follow the [Canadian Ice Service account of ice formation](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/ice-forecasts-observations/publications/sea-cycle/chapter-1.html).

[[Sea Ice in the Green Sea]] preserves two unadopted extensions: a more extensive northeastern seasonal freeze, or a low-ice arrangement in which submerged northern exchange admits cold water while limiting imported floes. Blocking imported floes does not eliminate local ice formation. Neither scenario assigns the undeveloped northeast a settled geography or ice boundary, and neither is required by the secondary cool maritime ridge in [[Green Sea Atmospheric Circulation]].

Early- to mid-May access to selected Ursk ports is a trade-model assumption, potentially assisted by local magic. Whether a particular delay comes from ice, storms, or both remains open. An access window for those ports cannot be extrapolated to all northern coasts.

The adopted ocean model retains rich western phytoplankton and marine life, supplied through river nutrients, winter mixing, recycling in shallow water, mixing around banks and passages, and localized seasonal upwelling. ## Local navigation

The fast coastal branch makes northwestern navigation difficult and local knowledge valuable. The same current that helps a ship travel west can carry it rapidly toward a shoal, divide at an island, or leave a vessel in an unexpected eddy. Local knowledge includes when to enter the current, which passages to avoid, and where to stand offshore. These conditions support exceptional Skaer seamanship.

[[Trading in the Green Sea]] develops route choices, sailing calendars, and commercial consequences.

## Physical weak points and parameters left open

- **Eastern exchange and its forcing.** The adopted two inflows and shared outflow need adequate channel capacity and a consistent sea-level and density field. Mixing the currents does not propel the return; the detailed Outer Ocean circulation and pressure field remain undeveloped.
- **Summer return flow.** Water balance requires continuing export, but it does not specify how much goes deeper, widens offshore, or passes through each eastern opening. Without those details, a weak surface arrow cannot be read as a calculated transport balance.
- **Heat budget and sea ice.** The model needs sufficient imported and stored heat to preserve its open-water bounds while retaining northern cold water. No measured heat balance or regional ice extent is assigned. A cool maritime ridge does not independently prove either sea-ice scenario.
- **Coastal geometry.** Stronger westward northern flow and western Cymean upwelling are mutually compatible for the stated shore orientations. Headlands, bends, banks, straits, and reversals can alter both; the model does not establish every local surface current from a seasonal wind arrow.
- **Productivity.** Nutrient delivery must reach sunlit water, and prey must remain or accumulate long enough to support rich feeding grounds. Cold water, mixing, or a front alone does not guarantee high productivity everywhere. Proposed banks and any planar assistance remain distinct from the established marine-bounty requirement.
- **Unassigned quantities.** Current speeds, widths, transports, salinities, temperatures, tide strengths, shelf edges, exact confluence position, seasonal shifts, port-opening dates beyond the trade assumptions, and the reach of magic. There is no numerical weather or ocean simulation behind the qualitative maps.
