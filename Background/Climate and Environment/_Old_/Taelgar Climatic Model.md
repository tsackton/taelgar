# Taelgar Climatic Model

> [!note] Archived climate material
> Preserved for historical comparison. The current continental overview is [[Taelgar Climate Model Revised]]; [[Regional Climate Atlas]] defines the current regional coverage.

## Purpose, coverage and standing

This is the shared climatic model for the developed parts of Taelgar: the [[Green Sea]] and [[Greater Sembara]], [[Greater Dunmar]] and its transition toward [[Greater Chardon]], and the western coast north to the [[Mawakel Peninsula]], with the adjoining [[Fiatara Mountains]], [[Erbalta Plains]], [[Lake Valandros]] and western Sentinel forests. It explains how these regions connect and provides a starting point for predicting climate in undeveloped places. It is not a complete global model.

The model describes seasonal tendencies and plausible weather, not a predetermined sequence of days. It distinguishes **required outcomes** established in play or adopted development from **model choices** about how to reproduce them. A required phenomenon remains required even when its explanation is open. **Working hypotheses** are explanations whose physical plausibility or local application still needs examination; **unspecified parameters** are details not yet assigned. A model prediction for an undeveloped place does not become independent evidence for the model.

Use the [[Regional Climate Atlas]] to find local profiles through Gazetteer regions. Detailed physical explanations have their own linked notes; [[Weather Patterns of Taelgar]] will connect the seasonal baseline to weather episodes, and [[Climate and Landscape Applications]] treats consequences such as farming and navigation.

### Climatic Constraints

[[Climate and Weather Constraints]] is the maintained register of accepted climatic requirements, dated campaign weather, supporting observations and exclusions. Required outcomes and their scope take precedence over the model's explanation. Weather on a particular date normally represents plausible local conditions, but can be exceptional; it need not set an annual average or fixed seasonal boundary.

## Physical framework and geography

The model uses broadly Earth-like rotation, seasons and ocean physics. The detailed regional systems share that framework without having to form one continuous pressure center. Present-day terrain controls the developed rain shadows; they should not be projected unchanged into the world before the [[Great War]]. Approximate latitudes are design anchors, not precise climatic boundaries.

> [!tip] Reading the pressure patterns
> A **high** is an area of higher air pressure than its surroundings; a **ridge** is an elongated area of high pressure. A **low** and a **trough** are their lower-pressure counterparts. Air tends to sink beneath a high, warming as it descends and making rain clouds harder to develop. Rising air can cool enough for water vapor to condense, producing cloud and rain where moisture is available. A low over dry country therefore need not be rainy, and a high can still have low cloud or fog beneath it.
>
> Pressure differences drive wind, but planetary rotation turns moving air. In the Northern Hemisphere, this gives clockwise flow around highs and counterclockwise flow around lows. Near the ground, friction allows some flow across the pressure pattern toward lower pressure. See the [Met Office explanation of wind flow](https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/learn-about/weather/how-weather-works/high-and-low-pressure/wind-flow).

Wind names identify where air comes **from**; arrows show where it travels. A northeasterly blows **NE → SW**, and a westerly blows **W → E**. A storm's movement is separate from the wind circulating around it: an eastbound low can temporarily bring westbound winds on its northern side. Ocean-current arrows describe moving **water**, not air.

Seasonal arrows and pressure centers describe recurring tendencies. Ridges expand, split, weaken and rebuild; seasonal migration does not mean an identical pressure cell travels between two fixed points. Mountains redirect winds and shape rainfall, but high terrain is not the same as high air pressure. Mountain pressure readings require adjustment before comparison with sea-level readings.

> [!tip] Why the subtropical ridge moves with the seasons
> Uneven solar heating drives a broad circulation in which air rises in the tropics and sinks farther poleward, helping establish the subtropical belt of high pressure. As the hemisphere warms toward summer, the tropical rain belt, subtropical highs, and midlatitude storm tracks generally shift poleward; they return equatorward toward winter. Land and ocean warm at different rates, so the belt breaks into regional centers whose positions also depend on geography.
>
> For northern Taelgar, **poleward means northward**. A place can therefore lie under a dry ridge in summer but in the path of rain-bearing storms in winter. A **storm track** is the region through which storms commonly pass, not one permanent storm, though in Taelgar there can be magical exceptions to this rule. This broad behavior underlies both the Green Sea high's seasonal shift and Chardon's winter-rain regime; it does not determine their exact timing or latitude. See [global circulation](https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/learn-about/weather/atmosphere/global-circulation-patterns) and the [subtropical ridge](https://www.bom.gov.au/resources/learn-and-explore/climate-knowledge-centre/climate-factors/subtropical-ridge).

Three maritime moisture sources matter to the developed system:

- The **western ocean** supplies coastal storms, the western forests and intermittent moisture reaching the northwestern plains. Its seasonal ridge governs the south-to-north transition from Chardon's dry summer to Mawakel's continued ocean exposure; see [[Western Ocean and Coast Circulation]].
- The **Nevos Sea** supplies Dunmar's monsoon. Seasonal pressure changes and terrain shape the contrast between wetter western/northern districts and dry interiors; see [[Dunmar and Nevos Circulation]].
- The **Green Sea** supplies seasonal maritime inflow to Sembara and fresh moisture for some winter storms east of the Sentinels. Its pressure systems and winds are described in [[Green Sea Atmospheric Circulation]]. The water circulation, heat supply and adopted Western Gulf exchange belong in [[Green Sea Ocean Circulation]].

The [[Fiatara Mountains]], [[Sentinel Range]] and [[Chataan Mountains]] are distinct barriers. Exposure, elevation and routes around their flanks matter alongside distance inland. The Fiatara shelter drier country toward Erbalta; the Sentinels separate western maritime weather from eastern Green Sea moisture; the Chataans mediate the transition between Dunmar's monsoon and Chardon's winter-rain regime. These broad roles do not yet supply complete mountain-zone climates.

## The connected seasonal cycle

Northern seasons provide a common frame. Dunmar also has its own useful sequence: a cooler dry postmonsoon season, a hot premonsoon season and the monsoon rains. The transitions below need not happen at the same time in every region or year.

### Winter — storms, mountain shelter and eastern moisture

With the western subtropical ridge farther south, ocean disturbances reach Chardon and the northern coast. Western mountains and forests receive cool-season moisture; mountain shelter reduces what ordinary surface flow carries into inland districts. Mawakel alternates snowy cold spells with maritime rain and thaws. Dunmar is in its cooler, predominantly dry postmonsoon season.

The working winter connection allows disturbances aloft to cross the Sentinels and help lows form or reorganize to their east. Those systems can draw a fresh supply of Green Sea moisture into southern Sembara. A low moving east can temporarily bring east-to-west surface winds on its northern side, so rainy or snowy episodes fit within the broader winter westerlies. The proposed southern storm corridor, its frequency and its reach remain uncertain. It must coexist with the Western Gulf's relative shelter and the colder northern interior. See [[Sentinels and Continental Weather Connections#Winter storms from Chardon to the Green Sea]].

### Spring — different transitions within the shared warming season

The western ridge expands northward, gradually reducing storm access toward Chardon while the Upper Coastlands retain useful spring moisture. Ocean weather still reaches Mawakel, and passing disturbances supply the developed spring and early-summer rain opportunities around Voltara and Erbalta.

In the east, the developing Cymean low brings maritime inflow toward Sembara before the northern Green Sea's midsummer wind reversal. Spring northbound and eastbound sailing opportunities therefore coexist with the beginning of Sembara's wetter season. Dunmar heats strongly before its main monsoon rains. These transitions share the annual heating cycle; they do not have to begin together.

### Summer — adjacent dry and wet regimes

The Chardon ridge favors dry summers in the south and extends along the Coastlands, ending south of Mawakel. Farther north, weaker storms alternate with settled spells. Variable inland ridge influence allows recurring western-ocean wet spells to reach Valandros and the western forests; their moisture does not come from a southern monsoon. Mountain shelter maintains drier country toward Erbalta.

Across the Sentinels, the Green Sea supplies Sembara's summer moisture. The Cymean low and surrounding ridges organize maritime inflow and offshore winds; recurring northern high pressure supports the northern westbound sailing season. The precise cause of that northern high remains a working explanation. Dunmar receives a separate Nevos-fed monsoon, while the Chataans mediate the transition toward Chardon's summer dryness. Neither monsoon is required to drive the other.

### Autumn — retreat, returning storms and regional lag

Western ridge influence weakens and stronger ocean storms return, although mild settled weather can linger around Mawakel. Sembaran summer inflow diminishes; the Urskan summer high breaks down and northern westerlies reopen autumn eastbound opportunities. Dunmar enters its cooler postmonsoon season as the rains retreat. Exact dates and the degree to which these changes coincide remain variable.

## Using the model and identifying limits

For an undeveloped place:

1. Find its Gazetteer region in the [[Regional Climate Atlas]], including neighboring profiles and their coverage limits.
2. Identify the modeled moisture source, seasonal wind tendencies, terrain exposure, elevation and distance inland.
3. Infer a provisional seasonal pattern at the level the model supports. Check relevant requirements and dated weather in [[Climate and Weather Constraints]].
4. Record which details are predictions or remain open. Keep any proposed magical departure distinct from both the ordinary baseline and established local facts.

Detailed Sentinel mountain regimes and a full Ursk/Great Northern Forest/Vostok model remain unmodeled. Other retained gaps include the tropical Shrev margin, [[Illoria]], the [[Blackwater Fens]] freeze–thaw regime, the Svinjo–Isenborg–Yengorza mountains, and the separate jungle regions near Drankor and at [[Orekatu]]. Local slope differences, adjoining northern grasslands, winter conditions and geographic boundaries also remain unevenly developed. The constraints register distinguishes those gaps from active conflicts; a new profile heading does not by itself resolve a gap.

Cross-regional open choices include inland ridge reach, the winter storm corridor, the strength and stability of pressure centers, transition boundaries and the timing of regional seasonal changes. Their owning physical and regional notes preserve the detailed questions. Qualitative climate development does not require rainfall totals, exact snowlines, fixed pass dates or numerical generator inputs.
