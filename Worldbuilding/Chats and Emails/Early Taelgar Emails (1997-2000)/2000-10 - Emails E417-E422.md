Hey Mike -- a few quick things.  First, have you decided anything about when you're coming down here, and if you're bringing anyone?  Either the 13th or the 27th is still fine with me.  Second, I've been thinking about religion in Taelgar a little bit.  I don't have a lot of time to write things up, at least until I get caught up in a little in my classes, but here's a list of ideas I'm thinking about incorporating -- if you've got any opinions, ideas, whatever, I'd like to here them eventually.  Oh, btw, I'm also done with the cosmology stuff except for pocket dimensions, which I'm still thinking about.  I'll send that out within the next week or so.

Ideas for religion:
-- monastically based
-- no weekly ritual or required day of service (like in Western monotheistic religions); rather, expectation of private individual prayers at certain times (before meals? morning? evening?) and special occasional ceremonies at monasteries; for more devote, perhaps "sermons" at monasteries around once a week: basically, my idea for daily practice of lay believers is based more on Buddhism and other eastern religions than western monotheistic religions; leaves two questions: first, how uniform should this be? (all over known map?, just in the "imperially influenced" regions? all human cultures?); second, what do the lay practioners get out of practice, esp if there is no afterlife of any sort?  perhaps some sort of afterlife is a good idea....although I think karmic rebirth, or something somehow similar is better than an eternal heaven or hell, which doesn't really seem in keeping with the divine philosophy of Araht/Melchior
-- ideology/theology of aspects vaguely similar to saints/bodhisattvas (Buddhist saints of sorts), in that there are patron "aspects" of certain things that can be very local or very universal, and that flucuate over time depending on which ones are given devotional attention
-- pilgramages should be incoporated, perhaps even common as a type of devotional practice
-- mystical/magical powers of priests need to be worked out, something along divine favor, but also some priests should be able to develop vaugely Kyr-like powers coming from access to the plane of thought, cf Buddhist mystical powers (mind reading, knowing past lives, sensing past associations of objects or places, clairvoyance, sometimes levitation or aspects of physical denial)

Major questions:
What is the point of human practice? Is there an afterlife, of what sort (eternal heaven/hell, karmic rebirth, something in between, or something radically different)?
How uniform is religious practice across Taelgar?  How much diversity should be contained within different local aspects, and how much should be contained within fundamental differences in practice or theology?
Should priests have multiple types of mystical powers, some developing from a sort of divine connection with a particular aspect or perhaps with Araht/Melchior itself, and some developing from intensive monastic practice, or should priests just have one or the other?
What is the relation between the lay practicioners and the priests, in particular to what extent does the church have political and financial power, and to what extent do they solely have spiritual power?

Any thoughts would be appreciated, although I likely won't get to writing any of this up for a couple of weeks.  It's something I've been letting percolate for a while, and it's starting to gel, but input would be nice before it gels completely.

Tim

What I mean is that the primary religious institutions should be residential monasteries, with temples only showing up in areas of large population density.  In other words, the religious locus of the community should not be a sacred space primarily used as a gathering place at particular times, but rather a sacred space that is set aside continually as a residential dwelling place for the guardians of sacred life, the monks.  As for how the common people obtain religion: 1) major ceremonies, 2) wandering religious orders, 3) alms-rounds by local monks, and 4) teaching sermons at monasteries ("dharma-lectures," to use Buddhist term, basically expounding of doctrine for a lay audience, far less ritualized than, say, a mass or some other religious ceremony like that).  To me, the following picture emerges: most communities have a monastery or a monastic community within a reasonable travel time from them, ranging from small groups of monks making their home in huts in the woods to small monasteries with 5-10 monks and a few buildings, to massive monasteries with 100s of monks, large monastic communities with attendent temples, meditation halls, lecture halls, etc.  In any case, most/all communities would have a sacred space set apart from the community proper in which rituals and devotions took place, tended by religious officials (monks) who lived within the sacred space and were a required element of the sacredness of the space.  Some of these monasteries would depend entirely on the local community for existence, going around each morning collecting alms/begging for food to support themselves.  Others would be self-sufficient, but still go on alms-rounds to spread the religion and acquire additional support.  There would also be wandering monks or monastic orders that did not have a permanent monastery home.  Finally, at least in the larger monasteries there would be teaching lectures to give interested/devote lay practioners a better grasp of religious doctrine.  However, the majority of practice for most people would be daily observances and alms-giving, both of which would be thought to keep the favor of God on you.  (Question: to what extent should this be true?  Do bad things really happen to people who don't give? Do good things really happen to people who do give?  See discussion below.)  Questions/comments?  Sound logical/coherent?  A particular question is the distribution of religious spaces -- in my description, I've depicted them as common and present in most communities -- and also the variety of religious spaces, which I've depicted as fairly high. An attempt to answer these, plus the question of religious diversity:

Divine Truth:
There are several levels of Divine truth, all of which are necessary for a full understanding of the nature of the Divine.  The most general level is an understand of the One, the Divine Soul, the World Soul, or whatever you call it -- the unified aspect of Araht/Melchior, the Deity that cannot be named or encompassed in words.  In some metaphysical sense this Deity is what ultimately exists, but in a practical sense this entity is basically incomprehensible.  The most powerful of monks gain incredible supernatural power purely by the act of experiencing this Divine Soul.  In some metaphysical sense, the Divine Soul and the Realm of Emptiness are one entity, although not really.  It is not possible for anyone but the most advanced practioners to even begin to relate to this Deity, and this Deity is never "worshipped" or understood as an object of worship -- rather, this is more like the ultimate truth of the world, understanding of the Ultimate Source.

The middle level is an understanding of Araht and Melchior as two separate entities.  On this level, Araht is understood as the Creator, Melchior as the Destroyer.  Neither is intended to have positive or negative connotations: creation can cause suffering as well as joy, and destruction can bring relief as well as sadness; both are necessary parts of existence.  On this level worship is possible, and temples or monastic orders devoted to Araht, or Melchior, or both do exist.  However, Araht or Melchior cannot directly intercede on anyone's behalf, so prayer on this level is not particularly effective -- rather, the "limited karma" I mentioned earlier is enforced and divinely ordered on this level, so actions of devotion balance the negative effects of immoral actions.  On this level, the world is understood as a process of constant creation and destruction.  Monks can derive supernatural power from this level.

The most specific level is an understanding of Araht and Melchior as being composed of an essentially infinite number of aspects.  It is at this level that popular worship thrives, because the aspects of Araht or Melchior can and do directly intercede (albeit rarely) on the behalf of lay practioners.  Monks can facilitate this process, and particular ceremonies and devotions facilitate it immensely.  There are many temples or monastic orders devoted to any number of the aspects of Araht and Melchior.

Religious Diversity:
Within this framework of Divine truth, the primary level of relgious diversity is the aspect level.  Thus most of the religious diversity of Taelgar is determined by what aspects and monastic orders are powerful in which regions, not by fundamental differences in doctrine, which is established as a Divine given.  There are some exceptions to this: first, there are some cults which are not divinely revealed, and not based on the genuine worship of an aspect of the Divine, although they may seem to be.  Second, human understanding of the first and second levels can vary depending on culture -- because the nature of the divine is inherently difficult to understand, it may be interpreted differently, although this would be the exception rather than the rule, and contact would tend to create homogeneity.  The popular religion of all cultures will have slight deviations from divine truth, which can only be fully understood by the most devoted and spiritually advanced monks, and thus lay practioners may perceive fundamental differences that the spiritually advanced perceive only as superficial.  Strange, foreign cultures (like the South, the far North, the desert people, or the secret Dunmari cult) may have strange, foreign interpretation as long as they conform on a fundamental level to the tripartate division of the Divinity from one to many, and the basic characteristics of those divisions.  Finally, non-human races have unique and different views on Divinity, in particular because of firstborn, and it is conceivable that some humans living in close proximity to other races would adapt some of the religious traits of the particular race they were in close proximity to.  How does this sound?

How the Divinity Relates to Humanity:
The "upper" levels -- the unified Deity and Melchior/Araht -- cannot directly interfere in the world, for in some sense they are responsible for making sure the world is maintained as a whole, for preserving balance.  They are in theory omnipotent/omniscient, and thus cannot act without disturbing the delicate balance of the cosmos.  They are, however, a source of power that can be tapped by advanced monks and other beings.  Therefore, the lay practioners can only relate to these levels through the intervention of monks or saints (understood to be humans raised to sainthood).  On the aspect level, however, miracles can happen, and do happen.  Because aspects are limited in there power, they can act without disrupting the balance of the cosmos.  Thus the lay people connect to the Divine through either the intervention of monks or saints, or directly through the aspects.  Side note: the concept of spirits, of the souls of the dead who could not escape or who were so powerful as to avoid being pulled away, is a good one I think, and in some sense could represent another way of utilizing the power of the Divine, albeit an often dangerous and unpredicable way.  Basically, there are three sources of supernatural power: the Divine, the Realm of Magic, and the Realm of Thought.  Eventually we need to figure out how all of those manifest themselves/can be manipulated from Taelgar, which if any astrology influences, and how they relate to each other.  For now, I think this is a good start on Divine power.  Second side note: I like the idea of names of God, that is one way by which advanced monks can access the power of the Divine Soul or Araht or Melchior, beings which are far more powerful than the aspects but cannot be directly prayed to.

That got a bit long, but I think it gets a bunch of ideas out there -- once we get these larger issues settled, we can start hashing out the nature of lay practice and monastic practice, and the actual monastic orders and such that exist in Taelgar.

That's all for now,

Tim
