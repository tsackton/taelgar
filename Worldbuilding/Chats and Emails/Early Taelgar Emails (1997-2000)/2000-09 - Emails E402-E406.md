That's a more complicated question that you think.  :-)  The short answer is that there is clear evidence of punctuated equilibrium in some lineages, but clear evidence of gradualism in other lineages.  In 1972, Eldridge and Gould published their paper on punctuated equlibrium that claimed that gaps in the fossil record were not due to missing information, but were in fact due to a punctuated mode of evolution in which long periods of statis are punctuated by short periods of rapid change.  The paper itself was a polemic against gradualism, and caused in turn an outpouring of papers by the gradualists claiming evidence for phylogenetic gradualism, in which species change slowly and continually over a long period of time.  The reality of the issue is somewhere in between.  There are definately lineages that show patterns consistent with punctuated equilibrium.  There are also lineages that show phylogenetic gradualism, and even lineages that show punctuated gradualism (long periods of statis punctuated by short periods of gradual change).  So the long answer would be that as a universal theory of the tempo and mode of evolution, punctuated equilibrium is not a sufficient answer, but then again, neither is gradualism -- there is no one universal mode that characterizes the fossil record and all macroevolution.  As a side note, punctuated equilibrium is not nearly as revolutionary as Gould likes to make it sound -- Ernst Mayr (whom I think is a far better thinker and evolutionist than Gould) came up with a theory of speciation earlier that is essentially a punctuated equilibrium model.  All Gould did was apply that kind of thinking to the fossil record, which admittedly no one had done, but which also didn't require a huge leap of intuition.

So that was probably more than you bargained for.  If your prof was refering to the fact that in some cases, punctuated equilibrium nicely explains fossil data, then he is right -- no serious and unbiased evolutionary biology would deny that in some cases punctuated equilibrium is a good model.  But if he was refering to punctuated equilibrium as the only valid and accepted model of macroevolution, he was wrong.

Certainly the Scientific Revolution was a historical event based on the particular circumstances of Europe at the time.  However, the fact is that there is a certain amount in inevitablity in the continuing quest to make models of the world that accurately reflect it.  Sure, there were a lot of factors that lead to the Scientific Revolution, but it could not have happened if the Aristotelean worldview of the late Middle Ages accurately reflected the world.  It was only when people started picking at holes that the whole structure fell apart.  And although the ways in which people picked at holes during the 16th and 17th century were historically bounded and lead to a certain scientific and philosophical results (mechanistic/materialistic philosophies, Cartesian rationalism, etc), the desire to pick at holes in explanatory models is a universal human phenonmenon, I would argue.  Eventually circumstances will arise that encourage the deconstruction of inadequate worldviews and the reconstruction of "better" ones (that have greater predictive/explanatory power).  It is hard to imagine that a culture such as the intellectuals of the Chardon academy would not be keenly interested in accurate models of their world.  Thus, if Taelgar actually functioned like our world does in terms of physics, cosmology, etc, we would need to make up ad hoc reasons for the Chardon intellectuals to not find out about, which seems hard and somewhat silly to do.  Does that make sense?

Hey Mike -- he's a basic overview of cosmology, with lots of questions, that I've gotten down.  What do you think?

Basic overview:  Taelgar is at the center of a multiverse consisting of: the realm of Taelgar itself, with associated lunar, solar, planetary, and stellar spheres (question: to what extent is astrology an important force?); the realm of the Deity, which fades from “pure” good at the very top to “pure” evil at the very bottom (symbolism: the gray areas in the center have by far the most “area”) (question: is this better or worse than the previous two sharply divided realms, which is still what virtually all scholars would think in cultures that perceive Araht and Melchior as independent entities); the realm of magic; the realm of consciousness, which includes the plane of consciousness, the dreamworld, and the astral plane; and the realm of nothingness (the “neutral column”).  The realm of the Deity is essentially inaccessible to living mortals of Taelgar.  The realm of magic makes occasional contact points with the realm of Taelgar, and is in very special cases directly accessible.  The realm of consciousness makes direct contact with individuals in Taelgar, and can be accessed with special and rare methods.  The realm of nothingness can be sought from either the realm of magic or the realm of consciousness, and it intersects the realm of Taelgar at or near the Temple (question: yes/no?).

Questions: elemental realms; lunar, solar, planetary realms and there correspondences to the races; fixed stars and constellations and the myths behind them (Beryl, for example); movement of celestial bodies; relationship of dragons to cosmology
