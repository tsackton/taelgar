Okay, this might stray a bit. I seem to have lots of time today (I had a
cancelled class), and I just got out of a lecture on temples in ancient
greece. So, some thoughts.

First, a basic timeline of Taelgar, to establish a baseline for the
drifting of culturals and the cultural diversity bit of this. I'm going to
mostly ignore non-humans in this, since except for lizardmen and
halflings, non-humans are a enough of isolationists that they will not
have a significant effect on human culture, with a few excepts (dwarves
and some human cities during the 2nd age, maybe elves during the 1st age)

0 (First Age): Humans awake
2865-2870: Great Wars. Much death. Many elves and humans die.
2871 (1 Second Age): Hkar raised. Tayldras created?
5401-5403 (2530-2533 Second Age): Great Wars. Much death. Hkar sinks. Many
                                  dwarves die.
5404 (1 Third Age): Talyrdras land on Nickel Island (does it have a real
                    name?). Most of Taelgar in chaos.
5550 (146 Third Age): Talydras land at Drankor. Beginning of Drankorian
		      Empire.

At this point the major cultural groups of the world are: the Drankorian
river valley peoples, streching across Dunmar and north to the middle of
Sembara; the northerns (Vasan plains, Damara, northern sembara); several
eastern groups (probably refugees to a large degree); Comor (relics from
the 2nd age--not much effected by the fall of dwarves); northern
"barbarians" on the west side of the mountains; several distinct groups
of Southerns (desert nomads, organized groups, organized desert groups,
whatever). Maybe a bit more. Anyway, as the Drankorian empire expands, it
consumes all of the people near drankor and several of the more northern
southern groups.

6618 (1214 Third Age): Plague. Fall of Drankor. End of Drankorian Empire.

So at this time, we have the Imperial groups (the original Drankor
empire/plains of Dunmar strain and the southern strain), plus imperial's
living more northwards in both Sembara and west of the mountains--both
mixing somewhat with the groups north of them, although less so for those
west of the mountain--the mixing is more likely to be war in that case.
North of Sembara we have the northerns, who occasionally raid south, but
are not terribly war-like. Along the Inner Sea, Comor has a culture
continuing back to the middle of second age (very old) and Damara, north
of them is another place of the northerns, although those in damara are
less nomadic and actually have a few cities. Along the south coast of the
inner sea are several cultural groups heavily influenced by but not part
of the Drankorian Empire. The further east one goes, the less the
influence. The south, who knows. I don't know what people want the South
to be like. Ideas would be nice.

6625 (1221 Third Age): Devastation from Plague tapers off. Pop. at 1/3 of
	               previous level.

At this point there are several major cultural groups in the world. In a
continuation of the above: the northern barbarians to the west of the
mountains have swept south (they would not have been heavily effected by
the plague) and have probably settled most of the land north of Chardon.
After a hundred years of two of raiding, they gradually settle down and to
a large degree become Amn. Dunmar is mostly empty, as in the Drankor River
valley. The two large remants of northern half imperials would be Sembara
area and Chardon area. Contact is probably maintained between the two for
most of the time, although the first few generatations after the plague,
probably not. Comor continues, as do the southern Inner Sea groups. Who
knows about the South, again. Ideas? Now, as time passed the groups move
around, interact, etc. (I'm going to assume that at no point is there
significant contact south of Dunmar--if anyone has ideas about the South,
great). To begin with, a bunch of northerns sweep south, conquoering as
the go. They take Sembara, but the conquest only lasts a generation (or
even a bit less). They settle Dunmar. So you have a northern group, with
very different culture from the Imperials settling dunmar. Sembara expands
as a kingdom, and although it develops differently then Chardon, maintains
communication and some large similartiy of culture. Well, let me skip to
the beginning of the fourth age. We all know what happened at the end of
the third. So, Chardon has expanded a bunch, mostly through population
growth. Sembara too--Arabor was a mixture of northern/Sembarans that was a
result of the conquest of Sembara, but it is gone and very few people now
live in the wilds between Dunmar and Sembara. But Sembara and Chardon,
although growing more different, are still essentially similar culturally.
Dunmar is northern--there are probably still some northern groups living
on the plains of Vasa. Damara at this point is a northern-based by very
distinct culture-Comor, although shattered by war and conquest, still has
a ancient hertiage, and its own cultural identity. The South is mysterious
as always (a side note on the Shontu--they left the contient at the end of
the 2nd age, found those islands, maybe even traded with Hkar). The
southern edge of the Inner Sea is at this point probably pretty culturally
similar--not sure what the culture is, but I imagine there has been a fair
amount of mixing among the groups out there. So we have 4 major groups
(Northern, eastern, southern, imperial) and several minor groups within
the major groups (dunmar/vasa/damara among northern, eastern gets stranger
as you go east, I imagine--most distinct but along the same lines,
imperial-Chardon/Sembara). Also, Comor, Shontu, and Icara are fairly
distinct. Icara is founded mostly by northern refugees of the Great War
(Volkraad, Amn, Voonlar) and is therefore a mix of Imperial (Volkraad was
mostly imperial) and the northwestern "barbarian" stock, albeit much
"civilized". So, that's my cultural diversity bit. I'll get to other stuff
later. And expect a somewhat cleaned up version of this, without the
historic derivations, to appear on the webpage soonish under the Human
link...

## E366 — “Magic and the South”

- **From:** Mike Sackton

Okay, here is my basic mechanics for magic, see what you think:

[Humans only, divine magic mentioned at the end]

There are several sorts of mages: those who can cast spells, but have no
particular aptitude for it (new advantage: Can Cast Spells: 9 points),
and those who have an aptitude for it (magery 1 through 3, 7 points each).
This just gives a level for those who can cast spells which don't require
magery. Mage sight (the seeing of mana and magic items) is 5 points and
it's commoness is equal to the power of the mage. Few Can Cast Spell ppl
will have it, most magery 3's will.

On Casting Spells:

To cast a spell involves a ritual of some sort, and requires that power be
drawn from some source. For mages, that source is the plane of magic,
better understood as "mana" (I assume your average mage doesn't know about
the plane of magic, and just draws from what seeps through to Taelgar).
This is represented by the mana stat, which is essentially IQ+Magery. Mana
is regained at a rate of 1/hour, which can be quickened with recover
strength. Furthermore, exhaustion makes it harder to use mana--for every
two points of fatigue lost, one point of mana is lost. So, casting a spell
requires a certain amount of energy, and a ritual. The energy is drawn
from personal mana, which is in turn drawn from Taelgar. The skill roll to
cast the spell remains at it would in GURPS. The only difference is that
is it possible to put only 0.5 points in a spell. However, that roll is an
aglamation of two rolls: the drawing and using of the energy roll, and the
successfully performing the ritual roll. If the two rolls were to be
spilt, the first one would be equal to IQ+Magery as a normal skill, the
second would be IQ skill with full bonuses from Eidetic memory. So, to
determine the complexity of the spell ritual, one looks only at what the
second skill would be. This is generally simply skill level minus Magery.
Now, here is where my indecision is:

System 1:

To determine the difficulty of the ritual, simply use the spell
level-Magery, and index it into a table of "difficulties" based on the
skill level, and perhaps the number of similar spells the mage knows.

System 2:

Make it dependent somehow on amount of points spent. So, instead of skill
level, all that matters is whether the skill is at IQ-5, IQ-4, IQ-3, ...
IQ+5, etc. The problem with this is that high IQ people are not
rewarded... I don't know if I care, though. Some artificial benefit could
be construed for high IQs like for knowing lots of spells.

So, I prefer System 2. Now, the difficulty table, so to speak. I'm
thinking something along these lines:

-5 or greater: takes many times longer than usual, and requires distinct
material, verbal, and somantic components (yeah, terms from D&D but I
don't know better ones). generally costs a lot more as well, possibly
draining large portions of mana.

-4 or -3: takes enough time longer than usual that the spell is distinctly
less useful, and it is hard to hide the casting of the spell. Doesn't
necessarily require materials, but doesn't require a noticably ritual.
Costs are similar to "what they should be", but higher costs are common
for better spells.

-2 or -1: similar to in GURPS Magic. Maybe twice as longer, same cost,
noticably but not necessarily difficult ritual

and so on. Another thought: the complexity of the spell should really
effect this, more than just having a class of M/H and some M/VH. Not sure
how to make that mechanic work. Maybe take the number of preqs in GURPS
Magic as a sign of complexity and give a penality to the above proportial
to the number of preqs? Or just the number of unforfilled preqs? Or
something along those lines. Also, it should be easier to perform rituals
if you have similar spells. Some sort of bonus is appropriate if a mage
knows, say, 5 or 10 or 20 spells of a similar college.

Customization: rituals can be lenghtened to reduce the cost of the spell,
or shortened to increase the cost of the spell. Not sure about the
relationship, but I think it should be fairly high, especially the cost
increases for shortened rituals. Exponential might be a bit much, but
definetly not linear.

Thoughts?

On Learning Spells:

Spells are learned from a grimore or teacher. These are generally few and
far between, and somewhat secretive and mysterious. It is fairly simple to
cast a spell once you are comfortable with using magical energy in
different basic ways. It just requires the ritual after that. Rituals can
be learned at a "default" (IQ-5) or so level quickly, and improved upon
after that. The improvement is just like the improvement of other skills.
Learning the manipulation of magical energy is hard, however. That takes
the full 200 or more hours, and dedication and a teacher of some sort. It
can't be learned from a book. Basically after enough spells, someone can
pick up others fairly quickly. But getting started is hard. The ritual
that one learns when one learns a spell is generally long. As the spell is
learned, that ritual is shortened in an indiviual way. So it is often very
difficult to tell what spell is being cast based on the ritual, even if
the mage knows the very same spell. Finally, as spells are learned,
everyone changes slightly the ritual and flavor, so to speak, of magical
energy used to create the effect. This gives spells slightly different
effects.

On Divine Magic:

Similar to the above, except that there are no spells, only effects and
rituals. Some rituals are well known, like a simple bless or perhaps
healing rituals--or rather, well known within the clergy. Some are created
on the spot. Creating a ritual isn't terribly difficult, but it often will
not do what the priest wants, at least, not without experience at creating
rituals. Divine rituals tend to take on the order of hours, occasionally
less, and very occasionally, a lot less (seconds, even). [Mechanics:
player says, I want this effect with this ritual, GM says, okay, that will
work or you don't think that will work, the player makes a holy ritual
roll and a "casting" roll -- if both succeed, the effect happens, if only
the first succeeds, nothing happens, if only the second succeeds,
something happens, just not what the player wanted, if both fail nothing
happens. The holy ritual skill is a M/VH that defaults from and has a
default to Performance/Ritual-4 or so. The casting roll is IQ+abritrary
bonus based on how pious, good, etc the priest is--generally +1 to +4 for
average priests, sometimes higher. A well-thought out or standard ritual
can give higher bonuses to this role as well.

One very quick comment about magic.  Make sure your players are aware that they can get _huge_ benefits if they actually write and role-play doing a ritual.  Note that most rituals of this sort should take several days to carry out, and require some interesting and maybe hard to get components.  But the benefits should be enormous -- for example, a complicated ritual requiring a week of preparation and a day and night to complete could allow an relatively untrained mage to do something really impressive, like maybe call forth a lightning storm to smite an enemy army or something.  Long, involved ritual magic should be _powerful_ in the extreme.  But so it doesn't get abused, don't let your players carry out long complicated rituals without making them up first.
