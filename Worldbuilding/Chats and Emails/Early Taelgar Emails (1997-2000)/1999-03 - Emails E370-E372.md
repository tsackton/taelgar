To give you an example, it took me about 4 hours of writing time to come up with that spell, and another 3-4 hours of researching.  Your players would have to probably put in about that much to come up with a good ritual, so that's a good 8 hours of real life time.  Anyone who does that should get some good effects.  Also, remember that this has to be roleplayed too, so that's another significant amount of time, maybe even a whole session, getting the stuff needed for a complicated and powerful ritual, and also carrying out the ritual.  And remember that there are always substantial risks associated with powerful magic, because you can attract the attention of evil entities.  So I don't think it's out of line at all.  As for invoking gods; well, religious rituals should definitely invoke different aspects/names of Aru and/or Melkor, depending on the effect you are trying to create.

Side note about religion, specifically religious power and religious magic:
I think the cultural aspects of religion are fairly well under control, although I will add this comment about Aru and Melkor -- what their actual philosophies are is very complicated and doesn't matter so much as how people perceive them.  The way I see it, people perceive Aru as a creator figure, a basically benevolent god, vaguely similar to the way the Judeo-Christian god is perceived.  People perceive Melkor not necessarily as evil, but just as something that should not be talked about.  Mentioning Melkor's name is thought to bring bad luck, and using his name is a magical conjuration is thought to have evil results.  Not exactly like Satan, because Melkor is not _evil_ per se; but he is seen as the one who comes and takes you away when you die, and the one responsible for sickness, and things like that, and naming him is thought to bring on those things.  Does that make sense?  As for gods as a power source -- I really like the idea of many, many names.  Here's what I'm thinking (and this is really just sort of an expansion of the aspect idea).  Both Aru and Melkor have many, many names.  These names are arranged in heirachical orders of power, with the "true names" being most powerful, then the names of the aspects connected with the firstborn, then the names of the aspects connected with other races, then any other variants on these things, or just other random names.  Note that here aspect does not mean the same thing it did before.  For the firstborn races, there is a name that is associated with the race that is the firstborn's link to the divine, and thus a very strong source of power -- there is also what the race calls its deities.  Note that every race, nationality, mythology, etc should have exactly two deites, polarized in at least vaguely similar ways to the human story.  For non-firstborn races, all aspect means is the second thing.  So Aru and Melkor are just the human names for these deities -- relatively powerful names, yes (equivalent to Jewish Adonai), but not the most powerful by any means.  Note also that this not only provides a way to have the deities act as sources of power without having to have them interfere (since the divine names have power in and of themselves), but that it introduces the concept of names having power in a solidified way into Taelgar, and thus could eventually connect to some scattered rules about True Magic users having true names (which I'll deal with when I finally get to dealing with magic in detail).  I know this is kind of incoherent, so see what you make of it, and then I'll try to clean it up.

As for other sources of power -- well, in an invocation, you have to get power from somewhere.  It can come from yourself or an external source, and that external source can be a deity or something else.  Personally, for mages I think the power should come from a non-deity external source, but for non-humans the power should basically come from within, augmented by the firstborn (but I imagine there would be a part of the power of that divine name in each member of a race, giving them internal power).  Humans, and other non-firstborn races, don't have internal power to draw from.  Human mages, however, can draw from the plane of magic -- so instead of gods, there will be some sort of astrological/mythical/alchemical/whatever-I-haven't-figured-it-out-yet invocations by which the mage can invoke the power of the plane of magic.  That's how I see it working anyway, and that's the main reason I want to make up the plane of magic -- so your players can write rituals about it.

Anyway, that's enough for this email.  I'll get to the South, and maybe some more quickish comments about magic later today, when I reply to your other email.

-Tim

Oh, one more comment re: sources for how to write rituals.  The best thing to do is to just read a bunch of rituals.  The Key of Solomon (trans. S. Liddell MacGregor Mathers) is a good place to start, plus a good web search can probably turn up a bunch of stuff.  Try to focus on the historical, not the modern neo-pagan stuff though, as I think that the modern neo-pagan stuff tends to feel less authentic for a role-playing game.  I have a feeling it tends to be less elaborate.  You probably don't really have time to do all the reading though.  If your players write spells, however, you can have them send them to me, and I'll send comments to you, and you can send them back to your players.

-Tim

## E372 — “Taelgar”

- **From:** Mike Sackton
- **Date:** March 15, 1999, 5:17 PM EST
- **To:** Tim Sackton

I have a few minutes so I thought I'd sketch out what Josh and I came up
with.

The northern people, the Belik [a name stolen from the Ebon people of
southeast Asian (useful making things up with an anthropology major :-)],
are organized along tribal lines. There are 7 or 8 traditional tribes and
a scattering of smaller families that are nominally part of a tribe but
are not in fact particularly cohesive. The tribes are often feuding
over water or cattle (the two most common reasons) or something
more esoteric. These feuds, although violent and sometimes truely hostile,
never really descend into actually warfare. The main reason for this is
the pun belik (lose translation: sacred people). The pun belik are a group
of men and women outside of any tribe, who are teachers, bards,
storytellers, and most significantly, mages and priests. Anyone with magic
is generally brought into the pun belik. The pun belik tattoo their arms
(either with snakes or with an indiviual animal--Josh's character has
snakes), and are inviolate. It is unthinkable that any beliki would harm a
pun belik. They are beggars, but they live better than most, because of a
tradition of hospitality. The travel around the northern part of the
Sarawak desert, teaching, learning, and maintaining the cultural identity
of the belik. Their magic tends to be in the form of chants--more on this
when I have more time.

One final note: I decided that two generations ago (maybe three), seven
gnomes (a sacred number to the gnomes) appeared at the annual gathering of
the pun belik (where new pun belik are made) and proclaimed a propechy
sayng essentially that, although by isolating themselves from the rest of
the world the belik survived the Great War, the aftermath of that war
would devasted them if they were not prepared for it. In order to better
keep a handle on the rest of the world, the pun belik began sending one or
two members north and south, to spend five or ten years journeying and
then return and report. Josh is one of these people.

More on the Belik later. A few really quick thoughts on Corwil:

I'm think of basing it on the medieval towns of Italty--essentially a
conflict between the privateers and saliors, and the urbane aristocrats,
ripe with secret societies, bloodfeuds, and complex politics. There is a
traditional position of Prince, which all of the towns and cities swore to
obey, but that position is often vacant, and very few princes live more
than a few years in office. More on this later.

Mike
