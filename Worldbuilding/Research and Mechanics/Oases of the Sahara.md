# Oases of the Sahara

At Chebika in southwestern Tunisia, the old village stands up on the rock. Its water emerges from a spring in a limestone gorge. An open channel carries the flow about half a kilometre to the gardens, where smaller branches deliver it to individual plots. A Tunisian survey counted 116 growers sharing roughly 25 hectares. Each grower held a right to a certain amount of **time**. A water keeper supervised the turns, and a *gadous*, a water clock, once measured them. A visitor could stand at the spring and see where the water came from. To understand how it fed a village, they would have to follow it to the last garden. [Tunisia’s participatory development plan for Chebika, especially §§ A.1.1 and A.2.4.4](https://www.environnement.gov.tn/fileadmin/Bibliotheque/Projets_en_cours/Ecotourisme_en_Tunisie/Projet_GDEO/4_1.pdf).

## Reading the oasis-group map

The red outlines in the Reddit map are groupings drawn by its author, u/Venboven. The broad pattern holds up. The map labels the Drâa in Morocco, the M’Zab in Algeria, and Ounianga in Chad, for example, and all three contain clusters documented independently. The outlines work as a guide to **where to look**. Their exact edges, the suggestion of four to thirty oases per group, and the idea that each outline encloses one culture are the author’s interpretation. The units being grouped vary even among these three examples. [Original map and author’s explanation](https://www.reddit.com/r/geography/comments/17sqrca/map_of_the_saharan_oasis_groups/).

| Group on the map | What the group looks like on the ground |
| --- | --- |
| **Drâa, Morocco** | Along the middle river, six named oases run downstream from Mezguita to M’Hamid. This is a chain of farming districts. Someone upstream can take water that a downstream grower was counting on. Farmers interviewed along the river identified unequal access between oases as well as between neighbors within them. [Field study and map of the middle Drâa](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/14/18/2932). |
| **M’Zab, Algeria** | Five fortified towns, or *ksour*, occupy separate hilltops in one valley. Each has a mosque at its highest point. The groves also had summer dwellings, so the settlement included both the closely built town above and places to live among the palms below. [UNESCO’s M’Zab Valley description](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/188/). |
| **Adrar and Tagant, Mauritania** | Groves follow wadis through rocky plateaus. A *wadi* is a watercourse that can stand dry between floods. Some Mauritanian groves are occupied seasonally, with people returning to pollinate the palms and again for the date harvest, the *guetna*. A grove can look almost deserted on one visit and be full of families on another. [Mauritania oasis project, regional agricultural survey, §§ 40–43](https://www.adaptation-fund.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/AFB.PPRC_.36.Inf_.3.-Proposal-for-Mauritania.pdf). |
| **Ounianga, Chad** | Here one map label covers two sets of groundwater-fed lakes about 40 km apart. Ounianga Kébir has four lakes and Ounianga Sérir has fourteen. Some are fresh enough for fish, others intensely saline. Floating reeds shelter parts of the water surface from evaporation. A blue patch on a desert map tells a traveler remarkably little about whether they can drink there. [UNESCO’s Lakes of Ounianga](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1400/). |

### Were most oases built as caravan stops?

The claim that nearly all oases are human-made depends on what is being counted. In physical geography, *oasis* can mean a patch where water sustains vegetation in otherwise dry country. Springs and groundwater-fed wetlands can do that unaided. In his fieldwork on cultivated oases, anthropologist Vincent Battesti uses the term for a settlement together with the irrigated land its people maintain. On that definition, human work is essential even when the spring flows naturally. The post gives no Sahara-wide count that establishes a percentage. Dating the domestication of the date palm likewise tells us about a crop. It cannot establish the date or location of the world’s first oasis. [Battesti on the two uses of *oasis*](https://vbat.org/article1047.html) · [UNESCO on Ounianga’s natural water supply](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1400/).

Caravan trade really did give rise to towns. Mauritania’s Ouadane, Chinguetti, Tichitt, and Oualata grew up to serve the crossings and became centers of Islamic learning. Elsewhere the sequence was different. On the Kasr Bounou plain in Morocco’s Drâa valley, archaeologists dated farming settlements to the fourth–eighth centuries CE. Irrigated fields and substantial mudbrick settlements expanded later, alongside medieval trade. In Egypt’s Dakhla Oasis, Ayn Asil already had a governor’s residence and an administration around 2350–2000 BCE. A modern map puts all these places side by side, though people settled them millennia apart. [UNESCO’s atlas of the Mauritanian towns](https://whc.unesco.org/en/urban-heritage-atlas/ancient-ksour/) · [Archaeological survey of the Drâa](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10134701/) · [Excavations at Ayn Asil](https://www.ifao.egnet.net/bases/scbalat/about?lang=en).

## How people make and maintain an oasis

**Dig the garden down to the water.** In the Souf around El Oued in Algeria, farmers excavate a broad hollow called a *ghout* in the dunes. They plant date palms low enough for their roots to reach the water table. The palms can then draw their own water. The choice of where to dig depends partly on the prevailing wind. Growers set up windbreaks of palm fronds and regularly carry out sand that blows back in. From the rim a traveler looks *down* into the crowns of a garden that someone dug. [FAO’s illustrated account of the Souf ghouts](https://www.fao.org/giahs/giahs-around-the-world/algeria-ghout-oasis-system/en).

**Bring groundwater out by gravity.** In Algeria’s Touat and Gourara, a *foggara* taps groundwater through a gently sloping underground gallery. Vertical shafts along its course give access to the tunnel. Where the water comes out above the gardens, it passes through a comb-shaped divider called a *kasria*. Its openings split the stream into measured shares, and channels carry each share to a basin or plot. A specialist measures the flow and the entitlements go in a register. An owner can sell or rent a share. When rights change hands, the channels themselves may need rerouting so that the new owner actually receives the water. [Field study of the Touat and Gourara systems, with diagrams](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13201-014-0195-5) · [Field photographs of the dividers](https://gse.vsb.cz/2014/LX-2014-2-32-39.pdf).

**Lift water out of a dry valley.** Rain falling on a rocky plateau can rush down a wadi and soak into the sand and gravel of its bed. Even after the bed looks dry again, water can remain within reach of a well. In Mauritania’s Adrar, well levels can rise after unusually wet seasons and fall over several dry years. At Ouadane, old walls mark out the garden plots. In the Echeylal grove, traditional wells use a counterweighted beam to lift water into the irrigation system. The dry channel, the well, and the walled garden are three visible parts of the same water supply. [IAEA basin study, Atar aquifer system](https://www.iaea.org/sites/default/files/raf7011_taoudeni_basin.pdf) · [UNESCO’s illustrated atlas, “Palm groves”](https://whc.unesco.org/en/urban-heritage-atlas/ancient-ksour/).

Low ground often helps people reach water, but a single elevation rule misses much of this geography. The water source, planted ground, and houses can all be at different heights. Water can also arrive from different eras. Much of the Nubian aquifer beneath the eastern Sahara receives very little recharge today. The Reddit thread’s estimate of another 1,000–4,000 years gives a misleading impression of local security. Pumping near a particular oasis can lower its water table enough to cut off its supply while a great deal of water remains elsewhere underground. The opposite problem also occurs. At Siwa in Egypt, excess irrigation water and poor drainage raised shallow water levels and damaged fields. Maintaining a garden includes giving surplus water somewhere to go. [USGS groundwater modelling](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70046429) · [Egyptian water ministry on Siwa’s drainage](https://www.mwri.gov.eg/mwri2/?lang=en&page_id=34022).

## What the settlement feels like

In the historic grove at Gafsa, Tunisia, you could pass under dates, then reach up for a pomegranate or an apricot, with grain or vegetables growing at your feet. Farmers use the palms’ high canopy to shade shorter trees and beds underneath. Small basins take water in rotation. A plot’s distance from the source figures in the local arrangements for its turn. Elsewhere a handful of trees could do much the same work on a smaller scale. In fourteenth-century Walata, Ibn Battuta saw watermelons planted in the shade cast by just a few date palms. [FAO on the gardens of Gafsa](https://www.fao.org/giahs/giahs-around-the-world/gafsa-oases--tunisia/en) · [Ibn Battuta on Walata](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/1354-ibnbattuta.asp).

In the old Mauritanian caravan towns, stone or clay walls give the street few windows. A low doorway opens into a family courtyard. A narrow lane might lead to the mosque and school, the storehouses and travelers’ lodgings, or straight out to the wells and groves. At the *guetna*, some of the dates are eaten fresh where they ripen. [UNESCO on the Mauritanian streets and houses](https://whc.unesco.org/en/urban-heritage-atlas/ancient-ksour/) · [Mauritania’s account of the harvest](https://www.adaptation-fund.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/AFB.PPRC_.36.Inf_.3.-Proposal-for-Mauritania.pdf).

In Niger’s Kawar oases, dates are also what households trade for cereals. Salt pans and small gardens provide other income or food. At Bilma, a 1906–07 account describes incoming caravans carrying millet to exchange for salt. Some camels carried grass for the other camels. Even a caravan arriving at a productive oasis might have to bring its own fodder. [Niger’s Kawar wetland survey](https://rsis.ramsar.org/RISapp/files/RISrep/NE1495RIS_1804_fr.pdf) · [Northern Nigeria report, 1906–07, p. 70](https://libsysdigi.library.illinois.edu/ilharvest/Africana/Books2011-05/3064634/3064634_1906_1907_northern_nigeria/3064634_1906_1907_northern_nigeria_opt.pdf).

The thread also says salt was once worth its weight in gold. In the western Sahara in 1352, Ibn Battuta gives actual market prices. A camel load of salt fetched eight to ten *mithqals* of gold at Walata, and twenty to thirty farther inland at Mali’s capital. The unit of gold was a small weight. What made the same heavy slabs so much dearer was where the trader could deliver them. [Ibn Battuta’s account of Taghaza and Walata](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/1354-ibnbattuta.asp).

For the [[Great Desert]], a small settlement could come into focus by following one household’s water from its source to the supper table. Where does it enter the garden? Who else has a claim on it? What does the family grow in the shade, and what must they buy when the caravan arrives? The [[2026-11#November 11 — Who carried the salt? Caravans, rivers, and oasis towns in the Great Desert|November caravan notes]] follow the traders beyond the grove.
