## November 9 — Coins as propaganda and silver as wealth

*Inspiration: Britain's Most Historic Towns, “Viking York” (episode 2), presented by Alice Roberts; the discussion of the Viking hoard near Harrogate. [Episode information](https://www.channel4.com/press/news/ep2-britains-most-historic-townsviking-york-clips-embeds-available).*

> British most historic towns about York. Talking about a Viking hoard found outside (harragate?). A few ideas - coins as propaganda which suggests could use diverse coins more to make world feel alive. Eg St Peter’s coin. Also consider D&D economy and the idea of just shifting everything to silver and/or dropping less treasure. Like a hoard of a few hundred silver coins is extreme wealth. Bracelets as stored wealth too seems relevant

**Research:**

- **The hoard near Harrogate:** The Vale of York Hoard, found in 2007, contained 617 coins alongside silver objects, a gold arm-ring, and a gilded silver cup, wrapped in lead. Its contents included a dirham minted in Samarkand and a French-made vessel: a small cache could embody an enormous trading world. The museum describes its owner as exceptionally wealthy, based on the whole assemblage, including its substantial bullion and fine metalwork. This is a useful model for a compact, consequential treasure rather than a direct conversion from coin count to wealth. [York Museums Trust](https://www.yorkmuseumstrust.org.uk/blog/beyond-jorvik-the-vale-of-york-viking-hoard-andrew-woods/).
- **Religious and political claims in a purse:** A St Peter penny combines the saint's name and a sword usually interpreted as his with a hammer often interpreted as Thor's. The combination gives a concrete model for coins addressing overlapping religious communities. [University of Nottingham, Northumbrian Viking Penny](https://emidsvikings.ac.uk/items/northumbrian-viking-penny/). The Vale of York Hoard also contains an almost-new coin proclaiming Athelstan “king of all Britons,” a claim tied to his expanding authority in 927. A new title or emblem appearing in ordinary change could make a political transition visible during play. [York Museums Trust](https://www.yorkmuseumstrust.org.uk/blog/beyond-jorvik-the-vale-of-york-viking-hoard-andrew-woods/).
- **Wearable, divisible wealth:** The Galloway Hoard's broad-band arm-rings were made from recycled silver, mainly coins, and could be cut up and weighed as payment. Coin, ornament, and bullion were different forms the same silver could take. A bracelet could therefore serve as adornment, savings, and a source of change; a partly cut band could show that its wearer had already needed to spend some of it. [National Museums Scotland](https://www.nms.ac.uk/discover-catalogue/how-viking-is-the-galloway-hoard).

**Economy design:** Two separate choices are worth keeping open: make silver the usual unit of prices and treasure, and reduce how much purchasing power adventuring delivers. Relabeling every gold price and reward as silver changes the feel but preserves affordability. Making a few hundred silver coins transformative wealth also requires choosing what they buy: months of household expenses, livestock, a boat, or the means to establish a workshop. Those benchmarks could guide treasure awards without requiring a detailed economic simulation.

**Taelgar connections:**

- [[Coin Ideas]] already proposes Chardonian coins bearing the magistros's portrait, and hobgoblin coins bearing Cha'mutte's mark or, more recently, clan marks. These older brainstorming ideas are a direct starting point for propaganda in circulation: an old imperial mark and a new clan mark in the same purse could suggest changing allegiances.
- [[Coinage of Sembara]] establishes common silver denominations, historical debasement, and mistrust of unfamiliar hoard coins. A discovery could have a recognizable ruler, a problematic purity, and a local nickname. The earlier [[2024-01-10 - Coins|coin discussion]] proposes using names and debased hoards as flavor while keeping accounting abstract; that offers a way to add this detail without tracking every mint separately.
- [[Dwarven Coins]] establishes trusted trade bars stamped with known weights of silver or gold. Wearable bullion could provide a regional counterpart: merchants weigh a bracelet beside a dwarven bar, while its owner also sees it as something to display or give away.

### November 9 follow-up — The Horn of Ulf and stranger treasures

*Inspiration: The same Britain's Most Historic Towns episode on York; the Horn of Ulf at York Minster.*

> Follow up. Horn of Ulf. Unique treasure - elephant horn! More exotic things in treasure hoards. Sometime too staid - the real world
> Has wild things like elephant tusk horns in York

**Research:** The Horn of Ulf is an eleventh-century *oliphant*: a horn carved from an elephant's tusk, about 75 cm long. York Minster places its probable manufacture around Salerno or Amalfi in southern Italy, whose trade with Africa and the Islamic world influenced its decoration. A lion attacks a deer or antelope, while a unicorn and other mythical creatures circle the rim. The material, carving, and eventual home in York bring distant places together in one extraordinary object. [York Minster's object description](https://yorkminster.org/app/uploads/2023/02/20230302-Horn-of-Ulf.pdf).

It also carried a claim to land. Tradition holds that Ulf presented it around 1030 as a “horn of tenure,” filling it with wine and placing it on the altar to dedicate his lands to the church. That suggests treasure whose significance survives spending or sale: a spectacular foreign object could also embody a grant, an oath, or a family's contested inheritance. [York Minster](https://yorkminster.org/app/uploads/2023/02/20230302-Horn-of-Ulf.pdf).

**Taelgar connections:** [[Grimbaskal's Hoard]] already includes a giant-sized hunting horn carved from a mammoth tusk and covered in scrimshaw, alongside a stuffed owlbear and an alchemical treatise carved on metal cylinders. These are useful precedents to draw on more often. The Horn of Ulf adds another possibility: give a striking treasure a journey and a social purpose, so its unusual material, foreign workmanship, and local significance each offer something to discover.

### How far did one caravan go?

A merchant could cross the Sahara with one caravan. In 1352 Ibn Battuta left Sijilmasa in Morocco with merchants from the town and reached Walata, on the Mali Empire’s northern frontier, two months later. At the last watering stop they hired a Masufa scout to carry letters ahead. Friends in Walata secured lodgings and came four days’ journey out with fresh water. A traveler who knew nobody could address the letter to a merchant with a reputation for helping strangers. At Walata the merchants deposited their loads in a guarded square. Ibn Battuta subsequently set out for Mali’s capital with three companions and a newly hired guide. [Ibn Battuta’s account](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/1354-ibnbattuta.asp).

Goods also changed hands and means of transport. In eighth- and ninth-century Sijilmasa, merchants bought gold from Amazigh traders and financed further caravans. An early sixteenth-century account describes salt leaving Timbuktu by canoe to Djenné, where the slabs were broken up so Wangara porters could carry smaller pieces on their heads to the goldfields. Egypt was within a merchant’s personal range, too. Ibn Battuta reports that merchants from Takedda, in present-day Niger, went to Egypt annually and brought fine cloth home. For Mali to Cairo specifically, Mansa Musa made the journey in 1324 on his pilgrimage to Mecca. His royal party is evidence for what travelers could do. The salt shipment gives a better picture of how an ordinary cargo could move. [Metropolitan Museum on the gold trade](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-trans-saharan-gold-trade-7th-14th-century) · [Nehemia Levtzion, *Ancient Ghana and Mali*, “Towns and Traders”](https://sahistory.org.za/sites/default/files/archive-files3/nehemia_levtzion_ancient_ghana_and_malibook4you.pdf) · [Ibn Battuta on Takedda, pp. 335–336](https://www.heroesandlegends.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/2015.62617.Ibn-Battuta-Travels-In-Asia-And-Africa-1325-1354_text.pdf#page=361) · [Al-Umari’s account of Cairo](https://www.bu.edu/africa/outreach/teachingresources/history/ancient-to-medieval-history/k_o_mali/).

### What came out of the ground?

The salt and gold came from different country. At Taghaza in the desert, enslaved workers cut rock salt in slabs, two to a camel. Dates came in from the north and millet from the south to feed the miners. Major goldfields lay well south of the Sahara, at Bambuk between the Senegal and Falémé rivers and at Bure on the upper Niger. A merchant could make a living in a town between the two, buying salt from one direction and gold from the other. [Ibn Battuta on Taghaza](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/1354-ibnbattuta.asp) · [Levtzion on the goldfields](https://sahistory.org.za/sites/default/files/archive-files3/nehemia_levtzion_ancient_ghana_and_malibook4you.pdf).

- **Copper.** At Takedda, enslaved workers cast copper from a nearby mine into bars in town. Thin bars bought meat or firewood. Thick bars bought goods including millet and butter. A traveler could spend part of the cargo on supper. [Ibn Battuta on the copper trade, p. 336](https://www.heroesandlegends.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/2015.62617.Ibn-Battuta-Travels-In-Asia-And-Africa-1325-1354_text.pdf#page=362).
- **Silver and lead.** Medieval Tamdult, on the Moroccan desert margin, preserves mine workings, lead-smelting sites, and coin molds with traces of silver. Archaeologists also found evidence for brass ingots. One mining district could supply several workshops, each sending a different product to market. [Archaeological study of Tamdult](https://www.hmsjournal.org/index.php/home/article/view/686).
- **Carnelian.** Farther east and earlier, Garamantian beadmakers in southwestern Libya worked red carnelian from the region. Fragments left in their workshops give a model for a desert export small enough to carry in a pouch. [Study of the Fazzan beadmaking sites](https://www.research.unipd.it/handle/11577/3156873).

### Where does the water come from?

The Niger brings rain that fell somewhere else. Rain in the Guinea Highlands feeds the river upstream of Mali. In the flat Inner Niger Delta the flood spreads sideways into channels, marshes, and pasture, and takes weeks to pass through. In 2020 the water was already falling at Mopti while it was still rising downstream at Akka and Diré. For neighboring towns, the same flood can open fishing grounds in one place just as herders regain access to grazing in another. [NASA’s observations of the flood](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/an-inland-delta-flooded/) · [USGS on the Inner Niger Delta](https://eros.usgs.gov/earthshots/niger-river-inland-delta-mali).

A small oasis can work at the scale of a single valley. In Mauritania’s Adrar, occasional floods can replenish groundwater in the sand and gravel beneath a *wadi*, a normally dry streambed. A well can draw on that water after the surface channel dries. In prolonged dry periods some shallow wells have had to be deepened to reach the rock aquifer below. For a map, a caravan might cross a dry streambed and then follow it to a garden whose well still has water. [Hydrogeological study of the Adrar oases](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236856891_An_assessment_of_the_groundwater_resources_in_the_western_margin_of_the_Taoudenni_basin_Mauritania).

### What might a small oasis town feel like?

In fourteenth-century Walata, Ibn Battuta saw people sow watermelons in the shade of a few date palms. The meal offered to the arriving caravan was pounded millet with honey and milk, served in half a calabash. At Buda in the Tuat oases, he describes a village with plentiful dates that imported grain, butter, and oil. People went out before sunrise to gather locusts while the cold kept them grounded, then stored them as they did dates. These are two quite different answers to what an oasis household could put on the table. [Ibn Battuta on Walata](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/1354-ibnbattuta.asp) · [His account of Buda, p. 338](https://www.heroesandlegends.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/2015.62617.Ibn-Battuta-Travels-In-Asia-And-Africa-1325-1354_text.pdf#page=364).

For the layout, Mauritania’s surviving caravan towns have courtyard houses clustered along winding lanes around a mosque. A lane might lead straight out to the well or the palm grove. At Ouadane, walls mark off individual garden plots. Traditional wells in the Echeylal grove use a counterweighted beam to lift water into the irrigation system. At village scale, the walk from someone’s front door to their plot would make a good first description of the place. [UNESCO’s illustrated atlas of the Mauritanian *ksour*](https://whc.unesco.org/en/urban-heritage-atlas/ancient-ksour/).
