### Mike Sackton <msackton@gmail.com> — Mon, 29 Jul 2024 18:22:36 -0400

So...

As I mentioned towards the end of the game, it took two and a half days to
march from Ashcombe down through the woods to Aslain with ~120 villagers.
You arrive in only minor disarray to quite the commotion in the town.
Marching up with a whole evacuated village causes lots of people to wonder.

We'll start the next session with the arrival in Aslain, but just because
it's relevant and seems worth doing by email, a side-note about the letter:

Shortly after you arrive a well-dressed man who says he is the night
barkeep at nicer of the two local inns approaches you:

"A... excuse me... sirs. It is most odd, a messenger came to my inn late
last night, and handed me this letter. He was confused, and couldn't quite
remember his name. But he was very clear that this was to be delivered to -
and I remember his words exactly - the Tyrwinghan paladin riding a strange
thing, and his straw-haired trickster companion, and his young page, and
their dwarven friend.

I think that is you? Although", and he looks at Graymalkin, "it seems very
ill to me to call your extraordinary mount a strange thing. The poor
messenger, I gave him a room to recover, he seemed so disoriented, but he
was gone when I awoke this morning."

He holds out the letter, which is a tightly folded square of paper tied
with a white and black cord, with two Sylvan sigils on the front.

Mike

Celyn notes - with his accent thickening for a moment, though not so far as to make him hard to understand - “*I* speak to you in Sembaran, but I’m obviously not from near here.”  There’s an odd note to his tone, like there’s a well-hidden edge here, probably not noticeable to the barkeep, but the party knows Celyn well enough to recognize there’s something going on there.

But if the man doesn’t track things like regional accents, he’s not going to get the information he was shooting for.  “Perhaps that gentleman might know him, yes, thank you.”  Then there’s a pause, and a little shift in mien, where he’s visibly trying to put on his more normal upbeat tone despite whatever it is about the letter that disturbed him so much; he slips back to the original line of conversation to say, “I am glad that you appreciate his magnificence,” with a gesture indicating that he is returning to the topic of Greymalkin.



(For general information: At this point, Celyn is largely out of the conversation until the party has time to converse more privately, which leaves space for either Viepuck to do his running social interference or Robin to say something.  While he’s managing good game face for strangers he is obviously perturbed.)

Izgil casts Dispel Magic, precisely targeting the spell at the charm of
forgetting. It is a complex spell, and whoever created this enchantment was

writing as well, and almost lets the dispel fizzle. But Viepuck's words of
encouragement help him see that this too, is a trick, a defense embedded in

his gut feeling and lets the dispel unravel the enchantment, leaving a
safe-to-read Sylvan letter behind. It is a poem, which Celyn reads,
rendered below.

I thank thee kindly[1] for taking all away,
The folk of Ashcombe from my sight removed.
For now in peace I plot, both night and day,
With none to bother me, nor be behooved.

My minions mourn, their prey now far from view,
Yet I, in quiet, can my plans perfect.
Their real task awaits, no risks to strew,
With naught but triumph's joy to soon expect.

Remember well the task that I bestowed,
Forget thy dislikes, let them fade to naught[2].
Forget my haunt, where I in shadow bode,
And glad[3] shall be my heart, with victory[4] wrought.

As soon as I do win, I’ll disappear,
Out of your hair, and free from heroes’ sway.
So, Cleenseau’s valiant, keep far from here,
For in your absence, I laugh[5] and play[6].

[1] this [thank thee kindly] has hints of sarcasm and hints of real joy
[2] this line and the next [about forgetting] are where the charm of
forgetting was embedded
[3] this [glad shall be my heart] has a emotion resonance of a kind of
gloating gladness
[4] this [victory wrought] has the sense of both victory in a larger sense
and also a specific victory over the party
[5] this [laugh] is read as a mean, mocking laugh
[6] this [play] is read a bit like say, playing with your prey before you
kill and eat it
