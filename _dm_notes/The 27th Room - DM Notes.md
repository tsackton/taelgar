## The Spore

Millenia Ago, a spore from a cosmic entity in the nothing beyond the cosmos drifted through a crack in the universe and took root in the Feywild. A Fey Lord who lived nearby took pity on the strange creature lost in a strange world. He called it the child and taught it how to survive in this universe, how to understand time, what dangers to beware of, and of the inherent power that it had to make and create.

By the time the teacher left for an evening party, the child had grown into a young person, eager to stretch itself and grow and create. Not perceiving the planes as separate things, it sent roots out into the material plane, and grew houses that looked like the one its teacher had.

When the first member of the Drankorian order Arithremos Lamperum followed those roots back to their source, the Child found its mortal soul so captivating and beautiful that it took it from the mortal and kept it to admire. It did the same for the next, and for the several which followed: collecting a hoard of souls like a child collects pebbles or a dragon collects gold – not understanding that in doing so the mortals died and were prevented from moving onward after death. 

When the Order sent a group in force to the Child, they could only see the value that it could provide to them as a soul-eater – it could keep their enemies from moving on to the afterlife or being resurrected. Unable to imagine it as a lost child, they called it the Unknown Thirst after its hunger for souls. The Order struck a bargain: they would bring it souls to admire and keep all to itself on the condition that it stay inside a house they built for it. Without understanding the ruse, it admired and played with the souls they brought while they built a prison of iron and magic around it. The prison was not to contain the souls – that was beyond their art – but to contain the Thirst who they believed would never let go of the souls it hoarded.

Discerning that the Unknown Thirst exists in all time simultaneously – its experiences its whole lifespan at once without past or future – the Order came to believe that no wall or planar boundary would ultimately keep it imprisoned: it would see the way out as soon as it was trapped. Indeed, it had already grown a tiny root out through one corner of its prison – a warning of what might come. The only way to keep it contained was to lock it within a constantly changing labyrinth whose shape the Thirst could not predict.

The wizard Katheras tapped the energy of the captive souls held by the Thirst to design and construct the House of 27 Rooms – an immense mechanism to keep the prison chamber moving constantly and unpredictably through time and space, powered by the captive force of trapped souls and the unpredictability of mortal choice.

Centuries passed, and the House of 27 Rooms became one of a handful of hidden centers for research and study for the Arithermos Lamperum, safely far from the material plane. They fed their enemies to the Thirst, patrolled the boundaries between planes, and studied the shape of the universe. The Thirst, obsessed with the souls it collected, forgot it was a prisoner.

Until, in the early days of Apollyon's rule, the Order was disbanded. Its head, the wizard Thalestria, terrified at the prospect of the prisoner’s escape, tricked a Fey Lord into sending a steady supply of mortals to keep the house moving. In doing so, she would keep the prison secure for as long as it would take to rebuild the Order. But she underestimated her emperor, and it has been 700 years since the order disbanded.

To the Lord of the Endlessly Ending, the deal was struck just before his party started to wind down – only a chapter ago in the narrative \-- when at the height of celebration, Thalestria compelled this treaty.

Years from now, the aged adventurer Hulda Grim, travelling through the Feywild, happened to learn of the Endlessly Ending’s treaty and volunteered to go through the door. Winding his way through the 27 Rooms, the central prison jumped constantly in time and space, until when he passed through that door, he was speaking to the Entity 400 years earlier in his past. The entity in its aspect as the Unknown Thirst had long since gone mad, but in its aspect as the Child, it wanted to go home, and discerned in Hulda’s greed and resentment a narrow pathway out of captivity.

The Child offered him one of its Dandelion houses in Sembara, and all the riches within. In exchange, Hulda would have to end the treaty with the Ending, allowing the Elder to grow across the planes again. Hulda climbed out the root from the prison into the year 1356, and deeded himself in the future the Dandelion House. In trade for certain items he had taken from the 27th Room, he engaged the Philosopher’s Guild to deliver the deed to a younger version of himself at the height of his powers, four centuries in the future.

## Hulda’s Deed

Hulda Grim of Ironwood Keep (7 fighter/3 thief) is the second son of a minor lord in Sembara who always dreamed of more. He left to seek his fortune as an adventurer in his twenties and travelled all around the Green Sea as a sellsword. He made and lost treasurers and acquired a minor reputation. Bitter, resentful. The best is behind and it wasn’t that great.

In his early 40s, staying at an Inn in Tollen, an elf who said he was an archivist and librarian approached him one day, claiming to have a deed of inheritance meant for him. Despite seeming to be hundreds of years old, this deed named Hulda, his house, his lineage, his birth date, and a number of noble deeds, including the slaying of a wyvern who had plagued the northeastern border.

It was a magical contract. It gave to him a “Dandelion House,” but warned him that locked in a room in the basement was his doom: and the household would never be his unless he stepped through and convinced the man on the other side to end a long-held treaty.

In exchange for some unimaginably valuable gift, the elf had promised the person who gave him the deed to pass it on to Hulda, and see that Hulda signed it. Making it a binding compulsion (a geas).

## Endless Ending

The Twilight Kingdoms are a dozen dozen houses, homes, and holes when the setting sun is just over the horizon. A small river wends it way through gently rolling hills – grape arbors and wheat at the large manors made from yellow stone and marble, with columnar yew, topiary, paths of crushed stone.

The Arithrimos Lamperum followed the roots down the road to their source, a promontory above a ravine whose bottom is in blackness in the twilight. There they built a prison around the Thirst – the 27th Room – and their always-turning manor that kept it gated off in time and space.

In the days of the Empire’s collapse, the keepers of the 27th Room knew the house would have to keep spinning, so they came to Lord Revel at the height of the party asking him to strike a bargain that any mortal who visited would be sent through the front door of the House of 27 Rooms.

They gave him first the things they found precious: books from their library; a bird that speaks every language of men; a map of stars that twilight had not yet revealed. But these were of little consequence to Lord Revel – every guest brings gifts.

Next they gave him stories of human revelry: of fireworks like rainbows above a city of towers and pyramids; of ten days of feasting that followed the emperor’s coronation; of bonfires burning through the longest night on floating cities in the endless ocean. But these were of little value to lord Revel – for the dozen dozen houses of the Twilight Kingdom had celebrations for which these is no word in mortal tongue.

In desperation, they challenged him to game of poetry – a game of naming – whoever could most beautifully name Lord Revel’s Estate. Lord Revel spoke the name as it had been before a bargain was struck:

wide lawns spread down easy  
hanging lanterns welcome you  
a party begins.

Lord Revel Beginning.

But the wizard, wise in Fey lore, guessed correctly that Revel was trapped by his own story, trapped by his own immortality, unable to imagine any other moment in the narrative, not knowing that the wine would spill, the guests slowly drift away. So the wizard spoke the name as it would become:

wide lawns gone askew  
hanging lanterns flicker low  
a party ending.

Lord Endless Ending.

And in so naming, the Lord of the house looked up and saw it was so. Empty glasses sat on every surface, the floor was sticky in places, already a few of the candles has burned out. The chill of evening was in the air, and at the river a small boat was already in the water, the rowers calling out farewells.

That is the moment it has been ever since. From time to time, the grandfather clock chimes and Endless Ending sends a mortal down the road. But whether that has been once or a hundred times, who can say? Despair hovers just beyond the edge of vision everywhere that Lord Ending turns his head: soon he’ll be alone in an empty, messy house; the best is well behind him now.

## The House of 27 Rooms

The house of 27 rooms was not built to be a labyrinth. It wasn’t intended to be confusing at all. It wasn’t even built as a house, originally. It was a magical and mechanical device intended to keep the central prison chamber untethered from the world around it – changing the physical and temporal location of the central chamber. To make it truly random, it relied on human choice. Each movement through a doorway between rooms would shift the x, y, or temporal coordinate. There was no way for the prisoner to know where or when they were, and the only way for them to get free is if there was enough time spent in a single location.

But from that origin, it came first to be a convenient safe house for the order to stay in off-world, and then a study-house to better understand what it was that was imprisoned. So the rooms developed over time…

“On the surface of a sphere, draw a grid of four-sided polygons such that there are 89 bands from top to bottom and 89 slices radiating out, covering the surface completely in equally sized spherical boxes. Now, onto this sphere, overlay a map of any plane whose topology is spheroid, and you have 7,921 locations on the surface of the sphere. Within each individual location square, draw a second 89x89 grid, choose any one of those locations, and, repeat the process depending on the level of specificity you need. In a plane the size of Taelgar, the first grid is roughly 80,000 square miles, the second is roughly ten square miles, and the third is about 600 feet on a size,  the fourth is about yea big, square (he holds his hand out about 6.5 feet on a side).

## Intro Summary

*(Because this is the season finale, the “previously on The House of 27 Rooms” is a little longer.) The story so far:*

The embittered adventurer Hulda received a 400 year old deed of inheritance, written in oddly contemporary language, to a manor hour at the wild northern edges of the Dutchy of Telham. There was one condition: go through the door in the cellar of the house and convince the Fey Lord on the far side to break a promise once made.

Lord Endlessly Ending had been tricked into his compact by the wizard Thalestria, head of the Drankorian order Arithermos Lamperum in the waning days of Empire. Each time a clock she gave him struck zero, Lord Ending would send a mortal guest at his party through the doors of her house. This compact held to the present, 700 years – though for the Fey Lord, it was merely struck earlier in the endless celebration he still hosts.

Hulda hires the party who travel to the Fey Lord’s revel, where Tarek steals an yet to be disclosed possession of the Lord, and the party meets guests from across centuries, including a curious Rahkshasa. The Fey is unable to break his promise, but asks them to bring him the key to the prison-door: if he breaks it and the prisoner goes free, his promise becomes irrelevant.

Joined by Menesthius, a member of Thalestria’s party she left behind, the party enters the House of 27 Rooms. Surrounding a central prison-chamber, the Order built the House as a magical and mechanical device to untether the central prison room from the world around it and keep its physical and temporal location constantly changing: if it stopped moving, the prisoner would quickly escape.

Each movement through a doorway between rooms in the house shifts the physical or temporal coordinate of the prison. In that way, the randomness of mortal choices kept the prisoner from knowing where or when they were. The steady supply of mortals by Lord Endlessly Ending has kept it in constant motion to this day.

The party makes their way through the House, fighting off fungal-transformed attackers – one of whom infects Menestheus badly – and realizes that Hulda’s servant who hired them was one of these same mushroom-men. They meet Kallepsies, an ancient dwarven wizard putting the final touches on a fungal golem-body into which she intends to transfer her consciousness and restart the Arithermos Lamperum. They collect a clock which gives the always-shifting year inside the central room. And finally, they come to the library and talk with a clockwork magic research assistant who they name Arrae.

Arrae helps them to understand that the House is powered by the energy of trapped souls prevented from moving on to their afterlife. They read from the Order’s “Allegories for the Initiated” odd stories about Hedgehogs made to kill, and monkey-trap prisons, and mindless things that imitate minds. Arrae writes a personal allegory for each of them.

At the prison-door the party sees three doors to the three different voices they have heard in their wandering: a child, a young adult, and a crazed middle aged man who appears much older. As they open the door to the child, Menestheus, now fully transformed into a mushroom man, attacks. But dodging him, they step through and close the door behind them, standing now in an utter darkness as the clock strikes zero.

## The Release

An un-thing made of bone and bramble, of tree roots and mold, shaped like a dandelion seven stories tall.

Its roots move and seek like pale leeches exposed to the light, unthinking, terrified, writhing away from the center and reaching through the boundaries of the multiverse like living, clawing things. 

A dandelion whose pale seeds, spreading to a puffed-out sphere, have the faces of everyone you have ever met trying to rip themselves free of the horror they know themselves to be. 

A plant whose leaves are tentacles of rot and bone and darkness that wrap around shimmering spheres of mortal souls which scream and scream without ever stopping. 

And you know it is not this thing, this is only the closest approximation a mortal mind can make of its true form.

And then it is a child again. Blowing a dandelion puffs, but each seed is a soul it has been keeping, and they fly straight away, going to a home none of you know but to which you will one day go, and are gone.
